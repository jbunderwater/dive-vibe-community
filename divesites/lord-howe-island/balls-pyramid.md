## Ball's Pyramid

Ball's Pyramid is the world's tallest sea stack — a monolithic volcanic pinnacle whose height is reported between 551 and 572 metres depending on the source (562 metres is the figure most commonly cited) — and Lord Howe Island's most celebrated advanced dive site, located southeast of the island.

## Overview

Dives at Ball's Pyramid are conducted around the base of the pinnacle, with two main areas: **Observatory Rock**, a jumble of large boulders with rock tops around 8 metres and gullies between them reaching 16 metres, and **South East Rock** (sometimes referred to as Sunken Rock), which features large, flat, stepped ledges. Surge can concentrate strongly in the shallows near the surface — as little as 5 metres — at South East Rock. Sources report overall site depths in roughly the 25–28 metre range (lordhoweisland.info cites an average of 25 m, prodive.com.au cites 28 m, and diveplanit.com's dive-site listing gives a top-to-bottom range of 5–25 m); this entry uses 26 m as a representative maximum depth. Visibility is typically 30–45 metres. Because trips depend on favourable weather and minimum diver numbers, Ball's Pyramid cannot be pre-booked — guests express interest on-island and are called if conditions allow. Advanced certification is required.

## Site Information

- **Location**: Southeast of Lord Howe Island — sources report the distance as anywhere from 20 to 26 km (23 km is the most commonly cited figure); about a 50-minute boat ride
- **Entry Type**: Boat dive
- **Site Type**: Wall dive
- **Difficulty Level**: Advanced
- **Maximum Depth**: 26 meters
- **Special Note**: Weather-dependent; trips organised on-island when conditions allow. Advanced or Deep certification required.

## Marine Life

Ball's Pyramid is one of only a handful of sites in the world where the Ballina Angelfish (*Genicanthus sp.*) can be observed by recreational scuba divers. This deep-water species is rarely seen at scuba depths, but at Ball's Pyramid the fish appear on ledge formations accessible to recreational divers. Schools of Galapagos sharks aggregate around the site — divers have reported schools of 60 or more individuals gathering near the 5-metre safety stop. Other regular sightings include schools of Violet Sweep, Amberjack, Kingfish, Silver Drummer, Rainbow Runners, and Trevally. Large pelagic visitors such as Marlin, dolphins, turtles, and Wahoo appear seasonally, and Spanish Dancers and Galapagos whalers are also reported. Slipper and painted rock lobsters shelter among the boulders and ledges, with sea whips and black coral covering deeper sections.

## Dive Profile

Two main dive sites: **Observatory Rock** is a boulder-strewn site — rock tops sit around 8 metres with gullies between the boulders reaching 16 metres, offering plenty of terrain to work through in a single dive. **South East Rock** (sometimes called Sunken Rock) features large, flat, stepped ledges where the Ballina Angelfish has been observed, with surge concentrating in as little as 5 metres near the surface. Currents are variable and require zigzag navigation along the formations. The upper sections see the densest fish aggregations. Free-water safety stops are common given the open-ocean setting. Sources do not agree closely enough on a precise maximum depth to give one beyond the roughly 25–28 metre range noted above, so plan dives conservatively and follow your guide's briefing.

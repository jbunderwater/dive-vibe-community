# Wreck C-53

An intentionally sunk Mexican Navy minesweeper that has become one of Cozumel's most diver-friendly artificial reefs.

## Overview

The vessel now known as Wreck C-53 began as the United States Navy's USS Scuffle (AM-298), an Admirable-class minesweeper, per Wikipedia's naval-history record. Sources conflict on where and when she was built: Wikipedia states she was laid down 4 May 1943 and launched 8 August 1943 by the Winslow Marine Railway & Shipbuilding Co. in Seattle, Washington, and commissioned 2 May 1944. Multiple Cozumel dive-tourism sources (California Diver, Triton Diving), by contrast, state she was built in 1943 by "Wilson Marine Shipworks" in Tampa, Florida. This is a genuine, unresolved conflict between the naval-history record and repeated dive-tourism copy — both figures are reported here rather than silently picking one.

Her WWII Pacific service is corroborated independently by Wikipedia and California Diver, both describing pre-invasion minesweeping duty at Lingayen Gulf (6 January 1945) and off Zambales and Subic Bay (29 and 31 January 1945). A separate dive-tourism source instead references the "Gulf of Leyte," which conflicts with and is less corroborated than the Lingayen Gulf/Subic Bay account, so that claim is not repeated here. She was decommissioned from US Navy service on 19 June 1946.

In October 1962 she was transferred to the Mexican Navy and initially renamed ARM DM-05, per Wikipedia and Triton Diving. She was renamed "Felipe Xicoténcatl" (hull number C-53) in 1994 — not at the time of the 1962 transfer, correcting a claim in earlier versions of this entry that conflated the transfer and renaming dates. The Mexican Navy's official designation is "ARM General Felipe Xicoténcatl." She is named for a 19th-century Mexican military officer who died at the Battle of Chapultepec during the Mexican-American War (1847), an era in which Antonio López de Santa Anna commanded Mexican forces. His rank is disputed even within a single source: some references (including the ship's own naval designation) call him a general, while Wikipedia's biographical entry on him describes a colonel posthumously honored — this entry notes the discrepancy rather than asserting one rank as settled fact.

She was decommissioned from Mexican Navy service in 1999. Sources conflict on the exact sinking date: naval-history references describe her as sunk to form an artificial reef in 1999 and stricken from the Navy list in 2000, while diver-facing sources (California Diver, Triton Diving) give a specific date of June 5, 2000. This discrepancy is reported rather than resolved by picking one date. She was intentionally scuttled off Cozumel's west coast near Chankanaab Park. Before sinking, she was reportedly stripped of hazardous materials and had holes cut to allow diver access, per dive-tourism sources.

Length is consistently reported at approximately 184 feet (56 meters) across sources, with Wikipedia giving a more precise 184 ft 6 in (56.24 m). Beam is consistently reported as 33 feet (10 meters). Standard displacement is not stated here: Wikipedia gives 650 long tons (660 metric tons) for the class, but no second, independent source could be found to corroborate that figure for this specific hull, so per our two-source rule for wreck specifications it is omitted rather than presented as confirmed. Depth is reported inconsistently across dive sources depending on which part of the wreck is referenced, ranging from roughly 10m at the shallowest superstructure to 24m at the keel on the sand — this entry retains 24m as the maximum depth. The hull has been colonised since sinking, with coral encrustation and sponge growth creating an artificial reef environment. The interior compartments are accessible to qualified wreck divers.

## Site Information

- **Location**: Northwest Cozumel, Quintana Roo, Mexico
- **Entry Type**: Boat dive
- **Site Type**: Wreck dive
- **Difficulty Level**: Intermediate
- **Maximum Depth**: 24 meters
- **Typical Visibility**: 20–35 meters (65–115 feet)
- **Current**: Typically mild; can increase in afternoon
- **Best Time**: November to April; accessible year-round

## Marine Life

The C-53 has evolved into a genuine artificial reef ecosystem. Large grouper (sources describe "black grouper" rather than the previously listed, unconfirmed "Atlantic spotted grouper") are seen around the structure. Moray eels are documented on the wreck, though sources describe them generally rather than specifically in the anchor chain housing or portholes. Schools of glassy sweepers fill the hold spaces, creating shimmering curtains of fish — this is well corroborated across sources; the previously listed "silversides" schooling claim was not independently confirmed and has been removed. Nurse sharks are reported as occasional visitors; a previous claim of "juvenile Caribbean reef sharks" could not be confirmed and has been removed. Turtles are reported as occasional visitors resting on the hull, though sources do not specify hawksbill or any other species. The encrusting sponges make the wreck as colourful as a natural reef. A previous claim of flamingo tongue cowries on sea fans growing from deck fittings could not be confirmed at this site and has been removed.

## Dive Profile

Descend to the sand beside the hull (typically on the port side where the mooring is attached) and orient yourself to the ship's layout. The deck is at around 16 meters, the sand at 24 meters at the keel. A full exterior circuit of the hull takes approximately 20 minutes, leaving ample time for interior exploration of the main holds and accessible passageways. Penetration of the interior's deeper sections is for certified wreck divers with torches only. Ascend along the hull to the deck level, then proceed to your safety stop in the water column.

## Entry and Exit

Boat access from San Miguel; the C-53 is approximately 10–15 minutes from the main dive piers. A mooring buoy marks the wreck. Entry by giant stride; exit via boat ladder after SMB deployment at the safety stop.

## Tips

- Carry a dive torch even for the exterior — the encrusted surfaces reveal far more detail with artificial light
- Certified wreck divers can penetrate the engine room and main hold; consult with your operator about current guidelines
- The C-53 is excellent for a second dive following a shallower reef site
- Look inside every porthole and opening for resident moray eels and crustaceans
- Early morning light entering through the open forward hatch creates beautiful interior shots

## Safety

Wreck penetration without proper training carries serious risk; the interior of the C-53 is accessible but proper wreck diving protocols must be followed. Maintain neutral buoyancy throughout to avoid disturbing the silt that has settled on interior surfaces, which can rapidly reduce visibility to near-zero. Do not exceed your certification level for penetration. Carry a torch whenever entering any enclosed space. Fire coral has established itself on portions of the hull.

## Photography

The C-53 rewards both wide-angle and macro approaches. The full-hull exterior shot from the sand looking up at the silhouette is a classic Cozumel wreck image requiring a fisheye or ultra-wide lens. The glassy sweeper schools in the hold create extraordinary backlit images when light enters from exterior openings. For macro, the encrusted hull surfaces are rich with nudibranchs and encrusting sponge patterns.

# Cantarell Sand Reef

Cozumel's northern Eagle Ray Wall — a dramatic drop-off north of San Miguel drawing mass aggregations of spotted eagle rays from December through February.

## Overview

Cantarell (also spelled Cantarel) is known locally as the Spotted Eagle Ray Wall, also called "Eagle Ray Alley" by some operators. The OSM name "Cantarell Sand Reef" is misleading — the site is a genuine wall dive, not a sand reef. The wall drops from around 12 meters at the reef crest to over 30 meters on the face, with strong to extremely strong currents that make this site suitable for experienced divers only. It is located just north of downtown San Miguel. A previous claim that it "extends southward along the same wall system as Villa Blanca" could not be independently confirmed and has been removed; the two sites are roughly 5km apart with no confirmed continuous wall linkage in sources reviewed.

The site's signature attraction is the seasonal aggregation of spotted eagle rays, and this is genuinely tied to this specific site (not conflated with a different location) per independent confirmation from reefsofcozumel.com and multiple ScubaBoard diver reports. From December through February, diver counts vary — reports range from roughly 13 to 20+ rays — congregating along the wall face at approximately 24–25 meters, with some reports extending the season into March. This is one of the most reliable eagle ray encounters in Cozumel's dive calendar. Outside this season, the site still offers a dramatic wall dive but without the ray aggregations.

## Site Information

- **Location**: North of San Miguel, Cozumel, Quintana Roo, Mexico
- **Entry Type**: Boat dive
- **Site Type**: Wall dive
- **Difficulty Level**: Advanced
- **Maximum Depth**: 30 meters
- **Typical Visibility**: 20–35 meters
- **Current**: Strong to extremely strong — treat as a serious drift dive
- **Best Season**: December–March for eagle ray aggregations; November–March for optimal conditions

## Marine Life

The eagle ray aggregations are the primary draw and are well documented for this specific site: spotted eagle rays, with diver reports ranging from roughly 13 to 20+ individuals, parade along the wall face at approximately 24–25 meters (80ft) between December and February, with some reports extending the season into March. This is corroborated independently by reefsofcozumel.com and multiple ScubaBoard diver trip reports referencing "Cantarell"/"Cantarel." Claims of nurse sharks, sea turtles, groupers, barracuda, damselfish, moray eels, razorfish, seahorses, and lionfish at this specific site could not be independently confirmed against site-specific sources and have been removed; these may reflect generic Cozumel reef fauna rather than confirmed Cantarell sightings.

## Dive Profile

Divers must enter as a group and ascend together due to the strong current, which can carry divers rapidly along the wall or create downcurrents — ScubaBoard divers have reported dangerous downcurrent incidents at ray-viewing depths (roughly 100–116ft/30–35m). Drop to the wall face at approximately 24–25 meters to intercept the eagle rays during season; 30 minutes of bottom time is typical with regular air, longer with nitrox. The current dictates the direction; do not fight it. The captain follows from the surface.

## Entry and Exit

Boat dive from San Miguel or northern zone piers. Strong current entry requires a coordinated group descent. Water-column ascent with SMB mandatory. Nitrox 32 recommended by local operators to extend bottom time at depth.

## Tips

- Nitrox 32 is recommended to extend eagle ray observation time at depth
- Visit December through February specifically for the ray aggregations — this is when the site is at its best
- The current can shift or push downward without warning; stay alert and follow the divemaster
- This is not a site for newly-certified divers regardless of depth — current management is the primary skill required
- The shallow reef crest (5–12 meters) is worth exploring before dropping to the wall

## Safety

Strong and unpredictable currents are the primary hazard. Downcurrents are documented by diver trip reports, not uncommon. In extreme conditions divers can be separated from the boat. Always dive with an experienced local operator familiar with the site's variable conditions. Carry and deploy an SMB — surface pickup in current can be difficult.

## Photography

The eagle ray aggregations in winter months are among the most photographically dramatic spectacles in Caribbean diving. Position yourself on the wall face at 20–25 meters upcurrent of the ray group, allow them to pass, and shoot as they swim past. Wide-angle or fisheye lenses at f/8–f/11 capture the rays against the blue water column. Current makes static positions challenging — practice neutral buoyancy positioning before shooting here.

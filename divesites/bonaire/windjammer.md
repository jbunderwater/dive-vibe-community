# Windjammer

*The wreck of the Mairi Bhan, an iron-hulled sailing ship built in Glasgow in 1874 and lost on a Bonaire reef in a 1912 storm — a deep technical wreck off the BOPEC terminal. Independent sources disagree sharply on the details of her final loss; see below.*

## Overview

The "Windjammer" is the wreck of the Mairi Bhan (also spelled Mairi Bahn), an iron-hulled sailing ship built in 1874 in Glasgow by Barclay, Curle & Co. She wrecked on a reef off Bonaire's northwest coast, in front of what is now the BOPEC oil terminal, in a storm in 1912. Beyond these basics, independent sources diverge substantially on what happened and on the wreck's physical details — enough that this page flags the conflicts rather than picking one version.

**Sources disagree on the cause of loss and casualties.** The Netherlands Cultural Heritage Agency's maritime archive (mass.cultureelerfgoed.nl) states the ship was carrying loose tar cargo that shifted in a storm, causing a dangerous list; the anchor failed to hold and she grounded on the reef on 7 December 1912 — no casualties are mentioned. NUMA (National Underwater and Marine Agency) instead describes the ship grounding in a storm while trying to reach shelter, after which her asphalt cargo caught fire from a kerosene lamp, causing explosions that killed 4 crew (28 survived). DreamWrecks gives yet a third account: an initial grounding on 7 December *1905*, followed by a separate hurricane in December 1912 that dislodged and capsized the already-wrecked hull; it cites researcher Maurice Coutts's theory that the aging captain may have deliberately run the deteriorating vessel aground rather than risk sinking at sea, and reports 2 deaths during a later salvage attempt (when a mast fell) rather than deaths in the original wreck event.

**Sources disagree on size.** Tonnage is given as 1,278 tons (Netherlands Cultural Heritage Agency), 1,315 tons (NUMA), and 378 tons (DreamWrecks, and several tourism sites) — a discrepancy far too large to be measurement rounding. Length is given as 230 feet (Netherlands Cultural Heritage Agency) versus 239 feet (NUMA, DreamWrecks).

**Sources disagree on current wreck condition and depth.** The Netherlands Cultural Heritage Agency places the wreck at 60 meters with the shallowest point (the stern keel) around 45 meters and a mast visible at just 12 meters. NUMA gives 140–180 feet (roughly 43–55 meters) with the ship resting on her starboard side. DreamWrecks gives roughly 200 feet (61 meters) on the starboard side, with a metallic mast marker at 30 feet (9 meters) and two further masts lying on the sand, entangled in rigging. None of the sources found for this update describe both masts as still standing upright — that claim, present in an earlier version of this page, is not supported by current sourcing and has been removed.

Given this, treat any single number below as one source's account, not a settled fact. This is a deep, current-exposed wreck restricted by its location off an active oil terminal, appropriate only for technical divers regardless of which depth figures are correct.

## Site Information

- **Location**: Northwest Bonaire, in front of the BOPEC oil terminal
- **Entry Type**: Boat dive
- **Site Type**: Wreck dive
- **Difficulty Level**: Technical
- **Maximum Depth**: 55 meters
- **Typical Visibility**: 20–30 meters
- **Current**: Often strong, variable
- **Best Time**: Determined entirely by sea state and permit availability — not a casual dive

## Marine Life

NUMA reports corals, crinoids, nudibranchs, margates, and groupers on the wreck; other sources mention schools of jacks and large barracudas in the surrounding water column. No source provides a comprehensive, cross-confirmed species list specific to this site — treat these as individual reports rather than a confirmed assemblage.

## Dive Profile

Sources disagree on exactly where the wreck's shallowest and deepest points lie (see Overview for the range across sources: roughly 43–61 meters). What is consistent is that this is a deep wreck dive requiring a multi-gas technical profile with a substantial decompression obligation — not a recreational no-decompression dive on any account.

## Entry and Exit

Access requires a BOPEC permit and an accredited technical operator with boat support — the wreck sits directly off an operational oil-handling terminal. Shore access is not feasible. Plan around terminal operations and sea state.

## Tips

- This is a technical dive: full trimix certification, redundant gas, decompression planning, surface support, and a guide familiar with the site are all expected
- Do not attempt on standard recreational training
- Confirm BOPEC permit status before traveling — access has been periodically restricted
- The wreck is heavily exposed to current; surface conditions can deteriorate quickly

## Safety

Depth, current, decompression obligation, and proximity to an active industrial terminal each contribute risk — and the conflicting source material on the wreck's exact depth and layout (see Overview) means divers should plan conservatively rather than relying on any single figure. Penetration of the hull is not advised without specific wreck-penetration training and dedicated equipment — the iron structure has been submerged for over a century, and reports describe masts and rigging lying loose across parts of the site, an entanglement hazard. Always carry redundant gas and SMBs.

## Photography

Wreckage and any standing structure photograph dramatically against open water for divers who can safely reach them. Strobes are essential at depth; ambient light is minimal at 40+ meters even in clear water.

## Nearby Sites

The Windjammer site sits offshore from the BOPEC area on Bonaire's northwest coast. Karpata, La Denia's Leap, and Carel's Vision are the nearest recreational-depth sites along the same coastline, but none are functional alternatives — they are wholly different dives.

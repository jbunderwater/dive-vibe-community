# Blue Bead Hole

A sand-and-grass "muck" dive to about 17 metres, famous for the cobalt-blue Dutch glass trade beads that current and wave action have concentrated in the seafloor — one of the only places in the Caribbean where the artifact you find is the one you're allowed to keep.

## Overview

Blue Bead Hole is one of Sint Eustatius's most singular diving experiences — a site renowned not for dramatic topography but for the extraordinary historical artifacts scattered across its sandy, grass-covered bottom. During Statia's commercial peak in the 17th and 18th centuries, when the island was a hub of the Atlantic slave trade and among the world's busiest trading entrepôts, cobalt-blue glass trade beads made in Amsterdam passed through in enormous quantities. These pentagonal beads were used as currency in the slave trade. According to a detailed history of the beads (lifeonthegoldenrock.wordpress.com), after emancipation many freed people gathered at the coastal cliffs and threw their beads into the sea as an act of freedom; centuries of currents and wave action have since concentrated many of the beads in sandy pockets along the coast, and they tend to surface after storms disturb the sand.

**Correction (2026-09-11 re-audit):** earlier versions of this entry described Blue Bead Hole as a shallow 1–10m shore dive where bead collection is illegal. Re-pulled sources contradict both points. Two independent local operators, Divertrek and Scubaqua, list "Blue Bead Hole" as a boat-accessible dive to roughly 55 feet (17 meters) over sand and grass — Scubaqua explicitly classifies it a "muck/historical" site. And on the legal question, Divertrek states plainly that the blue bead is "the only artifact divers may take home," a rule independently corroborated by uncommoncaribbean.com, which describes blue beads as "uniquely exempt" from the strict no-collection rule that applies to every other artifact on Statia (anchors, cannons, ballast, etc. remain fully protected and must not be touched or removed). The depth, entry type, site type, and collection guidance below have been corrected accordingly.

## Marine Life

Scubaqua's site-specific list for Blue Bead Hole is pike blennies, scorpionfish, mantis shrimp, and flying gurnards — typical of a sand/grass muck site. Flying gurnards are independently echoed in a separate general search of diver reports for the site. This replaces a previous generic reef-species list (brain/star coral, blue tang, parrotfish, sergeant majors, garden eels, flounder, green sea turtles) that does not match a muck-bottom site and was not confirmed by any current source.

## Dive Profile

A boat dive over a sand-and-seagrass bottom to a maximum of about 17 meters. Divers work the sandy depressions looking for blue bead concentrations, which shift over time as sand moves and are most productive after storms. A local guide who knows current concentration areas dramatically improves the odds of a find. A typical dive runs 30–40 minutes.

## Entry and Exit

Boat dive with Golden Rock Dive Center or Scubaqua. Entry by giant stride onto the sand/grass bottom; no shore access is described by current sources.

## Tips and Recommendations

Blue Bead Hole is best dived with a local guide who knows the current bead concentration areas. The beads are small, pentagonal, and dark cobalt-blue, and are typically found after storms stir up the sand. Unlike every other historical artifact on Statia, the blue bead may legally be kept if found — but nothing else at this or any other site may be collected. An excellent pairing with Crooks Castle or Double Wreck for a day of historical Statia diving.

## Safety Considerations

All artifacts EXCEPT blue beads are protected by STENAPA and Dutch law — do not touch, move, or collect anchors, ballast, cannon, or other wreck material anywhere on Statia. Visibility can reduce after rain events. Current is generally light but can develop through the day. Always dive with a buddy and follow standard boat-diving procedures for entry and ascent.

## Photography

The dark-blue pentagonal beads against sand make striking macro subjects; a close-up lens at 1:2 or 1:1 ratio captures the colour and any encrustation on older beads. The muck-diving critters confirmed at this site — mantis shrimp, scorpionfish, pike blennies — reward a macro-focused approach generally.

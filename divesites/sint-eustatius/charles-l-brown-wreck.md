# Charles L. Brown Wreck

A former AT&T cable-laying ship deliberately scuttled on July 25, 2003 as an artificial reef, now lying on its starboard side at 30 metres depth — sources put her length anywhere between 300 and 340 feet, but every account agrees she is one of the Caribbean's largest diveable wrecks.

## Overview

The Charles L. Brown began life as CS Salernum, built in 1954 at the Navalmeccanica shipyard in Castellammare di Stabia, near Naples, Italy, for cable-laying and oceanographic work. In 1984 she was sold to Transoceanic Cableship Co., a subsidiary of AT&T, and renamed Charles L. Brown; she continued repairing undersea cables until 2003. Statia acquired the retired ship from AT&T for a symbolic $1 in 2002, and after volunteers spent months cleaning her of contaminants, STENAPA deliberately scuttled her on July 25, 2003 to create an artificial reef — a date corroborated independently by DAN Alert Diver and Scuba Diving magazine. Accounts agree she was flooded and sank gradually over one to two hours; there is no independently corroborated account of any accidental cause, so this description treats it as a controlled scuttling rather than a mishap.

**Sources disagree on her exact length**: Golden Rock Dive Center cites 300 ft, DAN Alert Diver and Scuba Diving magazine cite 320 ft, Divertrek and Scubaqua cite 327 ft (100 m), and the ship's original 1954 registry (per the Atlantic Cable historical archive) lists 339.6 ft — so reported figures range from roughly 300 to 340 feet (91–104 m). Her registered gross tonnage (2,789 GT) appears in only one source and is not independently corroborated, so it is omitted here rather than stated as fact.

She lies on her starboard side on the sandy bottom at approximately 30 meters (100 feet), with the highest deck structures reaching to around 20 meters below the surface — confirmed by DAN, Divertrek, and Scubaqua. In the more than two decades since sinking, the ship has been extensively colonised by hard and soft corals, tube sponges, and encrusting organisms. The wreck is one of Sint Eustatius's headline dive attractions and the largest diveable structure in the island's marine park. Visibility is typically 20–30 meters. Multiple dives are required to fully explore the structure.

## Marine Life

Golden Rock Dive Center and DAN Alert Diver independently agree on one signature resident: a large barracuda nicknamed "Charlie" that patrols the wreck. Golden Rock also reports Caribbean reef sharks and cubera snapper here. Scubaqua and Divertrek add horse-eye jacks, yellow-headed jawfish, angelfish, sea turtles, and spotted eagle rays, with sponges and deep-sea fans covering much of the hull. (An earlier version of this page listed groupers, moray eels, lionfish, snapper-and-grunt schools, stingrays, and hawksbill turtles for this site — none of those are confirmed by current dive-shop sources for the Charles L. Brown specifically, and the list has been corrected.)

## Dive Profile

Divers descend the mooring line to the highest exposed deck structures at approximately 20 meters and work along the hull toward the bow. The wreck lies on its starboard side, so navigation differs from an upright wreck — the port side faces upward and the starboard side faces the sand. The engine room and interior sections are accessible with a torch. The keel and deepest sand level reach approximately 30 meters. Full wreck coverage requires 2–3 dives.

## Entry and Exit

Boat dive with permanent mooring maintained by STENAPA. Access via Golden Rock Dive Center. Entry by giant stride. Maximum depth of 30 meters classifies this as an advanced dive requiring Advanced Open Water certification or equivalent.

## Tips and Recommendations

The Charles L. Brown rewards multiple visits — a wreck this size (reports range 300–340 ft) is impossible to fully explore in a single dive. Because the wreck lies on its starboard side, plan the first dive to orientate yourself to the hull's new geometry before targeting specific areas. A torch is essential for the engine room and interior sections. The wreck photographs best in morning light. Carry a DSMB for the open-water ascent.

## Safety Considerations

Interior sections involve overhead environments — bring adequate lighting and do not penetrate beyond light range without wreck diving training. Maximum depth of approximately 30 meters limits bottom time; plan the dive to allow adequate reserve for a safety stop. Do not disturb the silt in interior sections, which can rapidly reduce visibility to zero.

## Photography

The scale of the vessel makes wide-angle the primary lens choice — fisheye or rectilinear 10–18mm captures hull structures, deck furniture, and winch mechanisms in context. The encrusted bow with a diver in the frame is a classic Statia image. Interior shots require strobe lighting to reveal the colour of encrusting organisms. Close-focus capability is useful for the resident jawfish and for approaching "Charlie," the wreck's resident barracuda.

# Charles L. Brown Wreck

A cable-laying vessel deliberately sunk in 2003 as an artificial reef, now lying on its starboard side at 30–31 metres depth with a 30-metre swim-through and heavy coral and sponge colonisation.

## Overview

The Charles L. Brown is a former AT&T cable-laying ship, built in 1954 in Naples, Italy as CS Salernum. According to the specialist maritime-history database atlantic-cable.com, AT&T Corp. (via its Transoceanic Cableship Co. subsidiary) purchased the vessel and renamed it Charles L. Brown in 1984. It was deliberately sunk in 2003 to create an artificial reef, purchased by Sint Eustatius for a symbolic $1 USD, cleaned of contaminants, and scuttled by STENAPA. Sources report the vessel's length as between approximately 320 and 340 feet (98–104 meters): dive-operator sources (Golden Rock, DAN, Divertrek, MyScubaDivingGearGuide) cluster around 100m/320–327ft, while atlantic-cable.com's ship-registry data gives 339.6ft. It lies on its starboard side on the sandy bottom at approximately 30–31 meters (100–102 feet), with the highest deck structures reaching to around 20 meters (65–70 feet) below the surface — confirmed by DAN Alert Diver, Divertrek, and Scubaqua. Golden Rock Dive Center notes a roughly 30-meter swim-through through the structure. DAN Alert Diver describes a debris field scattered in the sand below the hull. In the more than two decades since sinking, the ship has been extensively colonised by hard and soft corals, tube sponges, and encrusting organisms, transforming it into a fully functioning artificial reef that rivals Statia's natural sites in biodiversity. The wreck is one of Sint Eustatius's headline dive attractions and the largest diveable structure in the island's marine park. Visibility is typically 20–30 meters. Multiple dives are required to fully explore the structure.

## Marine Life

Multiple independent operator sources converge on a consistent set of sightings here. Caribbean reef sharks and large schools of horse-eye jacks are reported by DAN Alert Diver, Divertrek, and Scubaqua alike. Barracuda are a fixture on the wreck — Golden Rock Dive Center notes a well-known resident nicknamed "Charlie" — and Golden Rock also reports cubera snapper. Scubaqua additionally lists spotted eagle rays and yellow-headed jawfish around the structure. *Note: black/tiger grouper, moray eels, lionfish, snapper-and-grunt schools, stingrays, and turtles resting on deck were removed from this section during the 2026-09-11 re-audit — no current source confirms these specifically at this wreck.*

## Dive Profile

Divers descend the mooring line to the highest exposed deck structures at approximately 20 meters and work along the hull toward the bow. The wreck lies on its starboard side, so navigation differs from an upright wreck — the port side faces upward and the starboard side faces the sand. Golden Rock Dive Center notes a roughly 30-meter swim-through through the structure for divers with appropriate training. The engine room and interior sections are accessible with a torch. The keel and deepest sand level reach approximately 30–31 meters. Full wreck coverage requires 2–3 dives.

## Entry and Exit

Boat dive with permanent mooring maintained by STENAPA. Access via Golden Rock Dive Center. Entry by giant stride. Maximum depth of 30 meters classifies this as an advanced dive requiring Advanced Open Water certification or equivalent.

## Tips and Recommendations

The Charles L. Brown rewards multiple visits — the 100-meter vessel is impossible to fully explore in a single dive. Because the wreck lies on its starboard side, plan the first dive to orientate yourself to the hull's new geometry before targeting specific areas. A torch is essential for the engine room and interior sections. The wreck photographs best in morning light. Carry a DSMB for the open-water ascent.

## Safety Considerations

Interior sections involve overhead environments — bring adequate lighting and do not penetrate beyond light range without wreck diving training. Maximum depth of approximately 30 meters limits bottom time; plan the dive to allow adequate reserve for a safety stop. Do not disturb the silt in interior sections, which can rapidly reduce visibility to zero.

## Photography

The scale of the vessel makes wide-angle the primary lens choice — fisheye or rectilinear 10–18mm captures hull structures, deck furniture, and winch mechanisms in context. The encrusted bow with a diver in the frame is a classic Statia image. Interior shots require strobe lighting to reveal the colour of encrusting organisms. Close-focus capability for the resident moray eels and groupers.

## Duke of Sparta Wreck

The Duke of Sparta — officially the SS Aquila — is a 134-meter British cargo ship lying on the silty floor of Ambon Bay, sunk in 1958 during a Cold War CIA covert operation.

## Overview

The wreck known locally as the Duke of Sparta is the Italian-flagged cargo vessel SS Aquila, built in 1940 as the SS Duke of Sparta by William Gray & Company at West Hartlepool, UK, for Trent Maritime Co Ltd (operated by Stavros Livanos). The ship was sold to Grimaldi Brothers of Naples in 1951 and renamed Aquila. At the end of April 1958 — sources give the attack date as either 28 April or 1–2 May — she was attacked while anchored off Ambon City by an unmarked, CIA-operated Douglas B-26 Invader. The mission was part of Operation Haik, the codename for a CIA covert air campaign (confirmed independently by declassified-document research and multiple historical accounts) that supported the Permesta rebellion against President Sukarno's government by targeting foreign merchant shipping to frighten trade away from Indonesian waters. The identity of the individual pilot who bombed the Aquila is not established in the sources reviewed here; a separate, better-documented CIA contract pilot, Allen Pope, was shot down over Ambon in a different bombing raid on 18 May 1958, three weeks after the Aquila attack and nine days before she sank — the two incidents should not be conflated. Damaged by the bomb blast, the Aquila stayed afloat for about a month before sinking on 27 May 1958.

The wreck was not positively identified until October 2009, when divers penetrating the engine room recovered a maker's plate from one of the water heaters, tracing the vessel to her West Hartlepool shipyard of origin. Reported hull length varies by source: Wikipedia gives 441.1 ft (134.4 m) with a beam of 57.8 ft (17.6 m) and 5,397 GRT, matching indopacificimages.com's figure of 134m; starfish.ch instead reports 137m; coralia-liveaboard.com only states "over 100m." Two independent sources (Wikipedia, indopacificimages.com) agree on ~134m, so that figure is used here, but the discrepancy with starfish.ch's 137m is not resolved.

The wreck lies on a slope facing down the harbor gradient. Depth also varies by source: Wikipedia and coralia-liveaboard.com both place the stern at approximately 15 meters and the bow at approximately 35 meters; starfish.ch gives a matching 15–35m range for the site overall. Bottles-up-diving.com is an outlier, describing the stern at 12–15m, the foredeck at 32m, and the bow beyond 40m. Given three independent sources agree on a ~35m bow depth against one reporting 40+m, this entry uses 35m as the maximum depth, but divers should be prepared for the bow to run deeper depending on which account is accurate. The hull is intact and heavily encrusted with coral and sponge growth. Visibility in the busy harbor is typically 5–15 meters, improving somewhat on incoming tides.

## Site Information

- **Location**: Northern Ambon Bay, near the main harbor
- **Entry Type**: Boat dive
- **Site Type**: Wreck dive
- **Difficulty Level**: Advanced
- **Maximum Depth**: 35 meters
- **Typical Visibility**: 5–15 meters (harbor conditions)
- **Current**: Mild to moderate; tidal influence
- **Best Time**: Incoming tide for clearest water

## Marine Life

Sources confirm moray eels, frogfish, nudibranchs, shrimps, and juvenile fish such as boxfish and emperor angelfish on and around the wreck structure. The mast area carries dense hard and soft coral growth. Fimbriated moray eels have been documented on the top deck at cleaning stations attended by cleaner shrimp. Lionfish and scorpionfish use the encrusted hull for cover. No site-specific reports of manta rays, whale sharks, or reef sharks have been found in independent sources; these species are associated with Ambon's outer coast, not the inner harbor.

## Dive Profile

The wreck is best approached from the stern mooring buoy, which sits at around 12–15 meters. From here divers can work along the superstructure toward the bow, which most sources place at approximately 35 meters (one source reports it deeper, beyond 40 meters) — a depth suitable only for certified advanced divers. The interior is penetrable in the holds and superstructure but always requires a torch, line, and proper wreck training. Visibility and silt conditions make buoyancy control essential throughout.

## Entry and Exit

Access is exclusively by boat from local Ambon operators; no shore entry is possible. Descent via the mooring line is recommended given harbor vessel traffic. Deploy a surface marker buoy before ascending and coordinate pickup with the boat crew.

## Tips and Recommendations

- Bring a primary and backup dive torch — visibility drops sharply inside the structure
- Maintain strict buoyancy; the harbor floor is heavily silted and easily disturbed
- Do not attempt penetration without proper wreck diving training and equipment
- Schedule the dive for incoming tide to maximise visibility
- Book with operators familiar with the site; local knowledge of the mooring and descent route is essential

## Safety Considerations

Harbor boat traffic is an active hazard at the surface. The wreck's depth (bow at approximately 35 meters, with at least one source reporting beyond 40 meters) means strict NDL management for recreational divers. Interior penetration always requires redundant lighting, a guideline, and proper overhead-environment training — characterising it as safe or routine would be inaccurate. Never attempt deep penetration solo or without wreck diving certification.

## Photography

The encrusted mast and superstructure offer strong wide-angle subjects when visibility cooperates. The macro opportunities on the hull — juvenile fish, nudibranchs, shrimp on cleaning stations — are the most consistently productive. A torch is essential for revealing colours inside the dark cavities.

# La Dieppoise

A former French Navy minesweeper (ex-HMCS Chaleur, MCB 144), 46 meters long, deliberately scuttled on 19 January 1988 near Amadee Island. Sitting upright with the ship's wheel still in place, she offers interior exploration at accessible depths -- rear deck at 22 meters, front deck at 18 meters, flybridge at 15 meters -- though her wooden hull has deteriorated significantly since she was sunk.

## Overview

La Dieppoise was originally HMCS Chaleur (MCB 144), a Bay-class minesweeper built by Port Arthur Shipbuilding in Ontario: laid down 8 June 1951 and launched 21 June 1952. She was commissioned into the Royal Canadian Navy on 18 June 1954 but served only briefly before being transferred to the French Navy on 9 October 1954, renamed La Dieppoise, and commissioned by France on 13 November 1954. After service based at Brest and later in French Pacific waters (including Diego Suarez and, from 1976, Noumea), she was paid off on 9 July 1987. She was deliberately scuttled near Amadee Island south of Noumea on 19 January 1988 to create an artificial reef and dive attraction; the original scuttling date of 12 January was postponed due to Cyclone Anne. These details -- vessel name, build dates, length (46m/152ft), and displacement (390 long tons standard, 412 long tons deep load) -- are consistent across Wikipedia, the official newcaledonia-diving.com wreck page, and Michael McFadyen's dive log, with no conflicts found.

The wreck sits upright on the sandy bottom. Her condition has changed over time: McFadyen's 1993 account described the timber hull as "basically intact" though "already ravaged" above the waterline; more recent sources (newcaledonia-diving.com, JustGottaDive) report that the wooden hull has since deteriorated substantially, while the aluminium structural frame remains in good condition. The ship's wheel remains in place on the bridge, though its wooden handles have been eaten away by marine borers over the decades. The depth profile is diver-friendly: the rear deck sits at 22 meters, the front deck at 18 meters, and the flybridge at just 15 meters, making the upper sections accessible to less experienced divers.

The interior includes the wheelhouse, gangways, and main compartments, with dining fixtures reported still visible in places. This is widely considered one of the most accessible wreck dives near Noumea, though divers should expect a hull in an advancing state of decay rather than a pristine structure.

*A note on sources: Zentacle.com lists a conflicting sinking date (07/08/1996) for this wreck. That date contradicts three independent, corroborating sources (Wikipedia, the official wreck-dive operator page, and McFadyen's firsthand dive log) as well as the vessel's own recorded 1987 decommissioning, and is treated here as an error rather than a genuine historical dispute.*

## Site Information

- **Location**: Near Amadee Island, south of Noumea, New Caledonia
- **Entry Type**: Boat dive
- **Site Type**: Wreck dive
- **Difficulty Level**: Intermediate
- **Maximum Depth**: 26 meters
- **Typical Visibility**: 20-30 meters
- **Water Temperature**: 22-27 degrees C
- **Current**: Generally mild
- **Best Time**: Year-round

## Marine Life

The upright wreck structure has attracted large fish species including trevally schools that hunt through the superstructure, coral trout, and occasional turtles and eagle rays (newcaledonia-diving.com). McFadyen's dive log additionally reports sharks, barracuda, and sea snakes at the wreck. JustGottaDive and a separate New Caledonia dive guide both report moray eels and groupers, plus shrimp, cowries, bigeyes, and nudibranchs colonizing the interior surfaces. The hull supports established coral and sponge growth after decades of colonization.

## Dive Profile

Descend to the wreck -- the flybridge at 15 meters is the shallowest point. Begin with the upper decks and bridge area, where the ship's wheel is still in position. The front deck at 18 meters and rear deck at 22 meters are easily reached. The sand at the base of the hull is at approximately 26 meters. Limited interior exploration is possible for appropriately trained divers, though the deteriorating wooden structure means compartments are less open than in earlier decades. The multi-level depth profile allows you to start deep and work shallower as the dive progresses, ending on the flybridge for an efficient safety stop transition.

## Entry and Exit

Access by dive boat from Noumea -- approximately 40 minutes to the Amadee Island area. Entry by giant stride. The wreck has a permanent mooring.

## Tips and Recommendations

- The ship's wheel on the bridge is the signature photo -- arrive early in the dive while air supply is full
- The multi-level depth profile makes this wreck excellent for managing bottom time
- The accessible depths make this a good introductory wreck dive, but the deteriorating wooden structure means penetration should only be attempted with proper wreck training and equipment
- Combine with a dive at nearby Boulari Pass or Amadee Island Reef

## Safety Considerations

The interior is an overhead environment, and its wooden structural elements have deteriorated significantly since the wreck was sunk -- this is not a stable, "safe" penetration site. Do not penetrate beyond sight of natural light without wreck diving certification and appropriate equipment, and be alert to the possibility of loose or collapsing timber. The maximum depth of 26 meters to the sand is moderate but requires proper gas management. Maintain good buoyancy to avoid disturbing silt inside the wreck.

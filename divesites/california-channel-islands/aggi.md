## Aggi

Aggi is a historic wreck dive in California Channel Islands, North America.

## Overview

The Aggi is a Norwegian-flag, Scottish-built 265-foot steel full-rigger (ex-*Aspice*) that stranded on Talcott Shoal off the south side of Santa Rosa Island on May 4, 1915 while under tow. Her heavily encrusted iron hull frames lie across Talcott Shoal, mostly in 6–9 meters with some debris reaching 15 meters. The wreck has fully integrated into the reef ecosystem, and local dive operators report sheephead, lingcod, and rockfish holding around the structure. Santa Rosa Island's kelp forests frame the site.

## Wreck History

The Aggi was a 265-foot (81 m), three-masted, steel-hulled full-rigged ship launched in 1894 as the Aspice. By 1909 she had passed to Norwegian ownership under the name Aggi. On May 3, 1915, the vessel was under tow by the steam tug Edgar Vance, bound for Sweden from San Francisco with a cargo of 600 tons of beans and 2,500 tons of barley. The vessels encountered a storm and the Edgar Vance was forced to release the tow. Over the following day, shifting cargo destabilized the hull. On May 4, 1915, the storm drove the Aggi onto Talcott Shoal, one mile northwest of Santa Rosa Island. Captain Ira Eaton — who also rescued survivors from the SS Cuba eight years later — brought the Aggi's crew safely to Santa Barbara; the ship's captain, Anskar Olsen, remained aboard with his first mate until the vessel was declared a total loss. The wreck was subsequently sold to Universal Film Company and then to the Newmark Grain Company of Los Angeles for salvage. In 1967, one of the Aggi's two large anchors was recovered by Santa Barbara mariner Glenn Miller and donated to the Santa Barbara Historical Society, where it remains on display.

## Marine Life

Channel Islands Dive Adventures' site-by-site listing specifically credits the Aggi wreck with sheephead, lingcod, and rockfish, and a NOAA photo caption from the wreck site notes "a seabass nearby." Beyond that, no source consulted confirms further species at this specific site — the corynactis anemones, sponges, sunflower sea stars, spiny lobster, wolf eels, nudibranchs, cabezon, and garibaldi named in a previous version of this description were regional Channel Islands generalizations rather than confirmed Aggi sightings, and have been removed.

## Dive Profile

The majority of the wreckage lies in 6–9 meters (20–30 ft) of water across Talcott Shoal, with some debris reaching 15 meters (50 ft). The site is highly broken up after more than a century of Pacific surge. Iron hull frames and structural members are the main recognizable elements, now functioning as artificial reef structure thoroughly integrated with the natural rock.

## Entry and Exit

Boat dive only. Santa Rosa Island is approximately 34 miles southwest of Santa Barbara. Larger dive vessels (Truth, Pacific Star) are recommended for the crossing. Overnight liveaboard charters provide the best access. The exposed northwest position of Santa Rosa Island means conditions must be carefully assessed — the Talcott Shoal area can only be dived in calm seas.

## Tips and Recommendations

The Aggi wreck is best approached in late summer and fall when northwest winds ease. Visibility can reach 20 meters (70 ft) in settled conditions. Watch for surge at any depth — it can be substantial even at 15 meters. Currents can be strong and can develop quickly. Bring a dive light to illuminate the structural sections.

## Safety Considerations

Water temperature at Santa Rosa Island ranges from approximately 10°C (50°F) in winter to 16°C (61°F) in summer. A 7 mm wetsuit with hood and gloves is the minimum; a drysuit is recommended for winter and spring dives. Northwest winds can develop rapidly; always monitor conditions. This site requires intermediate or stronger dive skills due to surge, current, and potential for swift deterioration of conditions.

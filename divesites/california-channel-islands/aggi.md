## Aggi

Aggi is a historic wreck dive in California Channel Islands, North America.

## Overview

The Aggi is a Norwegian-flag, Scottish-built 265-foot steel full-rigger (ex-*Aspice*) that stranded on Talcott Shoal off the northwest side of Santa Rosa Island in early May 1915 while under tow (sources differ on the exact date — see Wreck History). Her heavily encrusted iron hull frames now lie across Talcott Shoal at around 6–15 meters. The wreck has fully integrated into the reef ecosystem, and Channel Islands Dive Adventures — a local dive operator — reports sheepshead, lingcod, small rockfish, and invertebrate growth at the site. Santa Rosa Island's kelp forests frame the dive.

## Wreck History

The Aggi was a 265-foot (81 m), three-masted, steel-hulled full-rigged ship launched in 1894 as the Aspice. By 1909 she had passed to Norwegian ownership under the name Aggi. The vessel was under tow by the steam tug Edgar Vance, bound for Sweden from San Francisco with a cargo of 600 tons of beans and 2,500 tons of barley, when a storm forced the tug to release the tow. Sources disagree on the exact stranding date: NOAA's Channel Islands National Marine Sanctuary page gives May 3, 1915, while Islapedia and other accounts give May 4, 1915. What is consistent across sources is that the storm drove the Aggi onto Talcott Shoal, one mile northwest of Santa Rosa Island, within that two-day window. Captain Ira Eaton — who also rescued survivors from the SS Cuba eight years later — brought the Aggi's crew safely to Santa Barbara; the ship's captain, Anskar Olsen, remained aboard with his first mate until the vessel was declared a total loss. The wreck was subsequently sold to Universal Film Company and then to the Newmark Grain Company of Los Angeles for salvage. In 1967, one of the Aggi's two large anchors was recovered by Santa Barbara mariner Glenn Miller and donated to the Santa Barbara Historical Society, where it remains on display.

## Marine Life

Channel Islands Dive Adventures, a local dive operator, reports sheepshead, lingcod, small rockfish, and invertebrate growth at the Aggi wreck specifically. Earlier versions of this description also listed corynactis anemones, sponges, sunflower sea stars, California spiny lobster, wolf eels, nudibranchs, cabezon, and garibaldi — none of these were confirmed by any source specific to this site during this QA pass, and they have been removed. Santa Rosa Island's kelp forest habitat generally supports species like these, but attributing them specifically to the Aggi wreck without a source would be speculative.

## Dive Profile

The majority of the wreckage lies in 6–9 meters (20–30 ft) of water across Talcott Shoal, with some debris reaching 15 meters (50 ft). The site is highly broken up after more than a century of Pacific surge. Iron hull frames and structural members are the main recognizable elements, now functioning as artificial reef structure thoroughly integrated with the natural rock.

## Entry and Exit

Boat dive only. Santa Rosa Island is approximately 34 miles southwest of Santa Barbara. Larger dive vessels (Truth, Pacific Star) are recommended for the crossing. Overnight liveaboard charters provide the best access. The exposed northwest position of Santa Rosa Island means conditions must be carefully assessed — the Talcott Shoal area can only be dived in calm seas.

## Tips and Recommendations

The Aggi wreck is best approached in late summer and fall when northwest winds ease. Visibility can reach 20 meters (70 ft) in settled conditions. Watch for surge at any depth — it can be substantial even at 15 meters. Currents can be strong and can develop quickly. Bring a dive light to illuminate the sponge and anemone growth on the structural sections.

## Safety Considerations

Water temperature at Santa Rosa Island ranges from approximately 10°C (50°F) in winter to 16°C (61°F) in summer. A 7 mm wetsuit with hood and gloves is the minimum; a drysuit is recommended for winter and spring dives. Northwest winds can develop rapidly; always monitor conditions. This site requires intermediate or stronger dive skills due to surge, current, and potential for swift deterioration of conditions.

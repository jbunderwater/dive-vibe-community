## S.S. Chickasaw

S.S. Chickasaw is a historic wreck dive in California Channel Islands, North America.

## Overview

The S.S. Chickasaw is a 439-foot C-2-type steel-hulled cargo freighter (ex-USS *Thurston*) that grounded on the south side of Santa Rosa Island on February 7, 1962. Sources disagree on how much of the wreck remains today: California Wreck Divers describes the hull as having "fallen into the sea, piece by piece" over four decades, and Channel Islands Dive Adventures — a local dive operator — states plainly that "nothing remains visible of the ship now." Divers should not expect large intact structure such as an upright boiler; scattered hull fragments and hardware across the reef, in around 3–12 meters, are more likely. Channel Islands Dive Adventures specifically reports large sheepshead at the site.

## Wreck History

The Chickasaw was a 439-foot (134 m), 6,131-ton steel American-flag freighter built in New Jersey in 1942 as a C-2-type cargo transport. She served as the U.S. Navy transport USS Thurston in both the European and Pacific theaters of World War II before being sold to the Waterman Steamship Company of Mobile, Alabama in 1946, demilitarized, and renamed Chickasaw. On the evening of February 7, 1962, bound for Wilmington, California, from Yokohama, Japan, the Chickasaw encountered 8–10-foot seas, rain, and dense fog south of the northern Channel Islands. The vessel went hard aground on the south side of Santa Rosa Island, just west of South Point, between Cluster Point and South Point. Sources differ on the exact cargo manifest: Islapedia and the Santa Barbara Independent list plywood, toys, dishes, and optical supplies; NOAA's current Channel Islands National Marine Sanctuary page additionally lists shoes and "at least one 1950s vintage Buick." NPS gives the cargo's total value as approximately $1.5 million. All aboard were rescued without loss of life by the U.S. Coast Guard. Sources also differ on the exact headcount: NOAA states 32 crewmen and 4 passengers (36 total), while Islapedia and the Santa Barbara Independent report 46 crew plus four elderly tourists (50 total). Salvage efforts removed the most valuable cargo, along with brass, copper, wiring, and electric motors. By February 18, 1962, the vessel was officially abandoned. In May 1962, a Ventura scrap dealer was arrested by Santa Barbara County sheriff's deputies for stealing remaining electronics and navigational gear from the wreck.

## Marine Life

Channel Islands Dive Adventures specifically reports large sheepshead (California sheephead) at the Chickasaw site. Kelp bass, rockfish, lingcod, garibaldi, cabezon, and wrasse appeared in earlier versions of this description but were not confirmed by any site-specific source during this QA pass and have been removed. The wreck lies in cold, nutrient-rich water typical of the exposed south shore of Santa Rosa Island.

## Dive Profile

The site extends from the surf zone down to approximately 12 meters (40 ft). Per California Wreck Divers, the hull has been disintegrating for decades, and Channel Islands Dive Adventures reports that nothing of the ship's structure remains clearly visible today — divers should expect a scattered debris field of hardware and fragments rather than large intact machinery. Depth and surge dictate how far productive diving extends on any given day.

## Entry and Exit

Boat dive only. Santa Rosa Island lies approximately 34 miles southwest of Santa Barbara. Larger liveaboard vessels (M/V Truth, M/V Peace) are the practical access route. The south-facing coast around South Point is more sheltered from northwest swell than the island's west end, making it more frequently diveable — but the site can still be surgy.

## Tips and Recommendations

Chickasaw is best dived as a secondary site on a Santa Rosa liveaboard trip, in conjunction with the Aggi or Goldenhorn. Conditions on the south coast are often better than the north or west, but check forecasts carefully. Large sheephead are the standout fish encounter here.

## Safety Considerations

Water temperature at Santa Rosa Island is typically 10–16°C (50–61°F). A 7 mm wetsuit with hood and gloves is minimum; drysuit preferred in winter. The shallow, surf-zone character of the wreck means surge is a significant hazard. The remote location requires diver self-sufficiency.

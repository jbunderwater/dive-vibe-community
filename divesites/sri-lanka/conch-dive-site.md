## Conch dive site

Conch dive site is a historic wreck dive in Sri Lanka, Asia.

## Overview

The SS Conch was a 3,555-ton British-built steam tanker, one of Shell's pioneering "Conch"-class bulk oil tankers, completed by the end of June 1892 by W. Gray & Co. of Stockton (dimensions recorded as 338.4 x 43 x 26.4 feet). While carrying a cargo of several thousand tons of bulk oil from Novorossisk toward Madras, she struck a rock off Akurala village (between Ambalangoda and Hikkaduwa) and sank. Two independent sources (archaeology.lk and the Dutch Cultural Heritage Agency's MaSS database) agree on the essential facts — vessel name, tonnage, builder, and general 1903 sinking — but disagree on the exact date, giving June 2 vs. June 3, 1903, and on the rock/reef's exact name (variously "Akurala/Akkurala rock," "Akarta rock," or "Parsi/Passa gala rock"); both discrepancies are stated here rather than resolved by picking one version. Multiple sources agree the vessel broke in two as she went down. One historical-archive source notes there was reportedly "clear evidence" the original 1903 disaster involved an explosion or dynamite, but this specific claim appears in only one source and could not be independently corroborated, so it is noted here as unconfirmed rather than fact. Separately (and not to be confused with the above), that same source and general wreck-diving accounts note the wreck has been damaged by explosions and looting during salvage attempts over the decades since it sank — a different, better-attested phenomenon than the original sinking cause.

Reported depths vary by section of the wreck — sources describe roughly 14 meters near the bow and up to 21 meters near the stern, where the hull rests on its port side, with the wreck now spanning roughly 100 by 35 meters across the seabed. The wreck is described by dive operators as well preserved, with the steam engine, boilers, propeller, and propeller shaft still identifiable, and penetration into the cabin reportedly possible; as with any wreck penetration, this requires proper wreck-diving training and equipment and carries real risk regardless of how intact the structure appears.

## Marine Life

Grouper and napoleon (humphead) wrasse are specifically reported at this wreck by more than one source, including PADI's own site listing. Other species named in some dive-blog write-ups (e.g., "queen angelfish," a species not native to the Indian Ocean) could not be corroborated for this site and are not included here, as they may reflect copy-paste errors circulating between aggregator sites rather than actual observations.

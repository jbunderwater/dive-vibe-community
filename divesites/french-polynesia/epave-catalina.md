## Epave Catalina

Epave Catalina is a wreck dive site in the Faa'a lagoon near Papeete airport, Tahiti, French Polynesia, featuring the submerged remains of a Consolidated PBY-5A Catalina flying boat, registration F-OAYD, deliberately scuttled in November 1962 to create an artificial dive site.

## Overview

The wrecked aircraft was a PBY-5A Catalina (construction number 1689), originally built by Consolidated Vultee for the US Navy and later acquired by RAI (Réseau/Régie Aérienne Interinsulaire), which operated a small fleet of Catalinas on Tahiti's inter-island routes, including the Tahiti–Bora Bora service, in the 1950s and early 1960s. **Correction from an earlier draft**: this aircraft was not a WWII-era Tahiti–Bora Bora service plane — RAI's Catalina operations date to the 1950s, well after the war. In October 1960, F-OAYD suffered a hard landing at Raiatea and was damaged beyond economical repair. It should not be confused with a separate, unrelated tragedy: a different RAI Catalina crashed fatally at Raiatea on 19 February 1958, killing 15 of 23 aboard — that aircraft is not connected to this wreck. After being stripped of recoverable parts, F-OAYD was deliberately scuttled in the Faa'a lagoon (at the "false pass," known locally as Moana Faa'a) in November 1962. Sources disagree on the exact depth: two independent diver accounts place it at roughly 12–21 metres (40–70 ft), while Tahiti heritage sources (Tahiti Heritage, Hiroa) give approximately 25 metres — treat the deeper figure as the likely maximum and the site as spanning that full range. The wreck sits alongside at least one other sunken vessel, a schooner (locally called "La Goelette"), a few hundred metres away, and a separate sunken Cessna light aircraft is also reported nearby. Lagoon visibility is commonly reported around 15–25 metres, and water temperature is 27–28°C year-round.

## What to Expect

Two independent diver trip reports (Hacking Family, SV Delos) describe the fuselage, wings and tail section as largely intact, with engines and avionics removed as part of the deliberate-sinking preparation; one account notes the cockpit is more stripped out with some holes in the fuselage. The interior is accessible through an open cargo hatch and the cockpit area, and divers report sitting in the pilot's seat. Confirmed marine life at this specific wreck is limited to two site-specific reports: spotfin lionfish sheltering under ledges, and schooling yellowfin goatfish. Earlier drafts of this page listed parrotfish, blue-striped snappers, stonefish, whitetip reef sharks, and turtles at the site — none of these could be independently confirmed for the Catalina wreck specifically and they have been removed. Buoyancy control is important in the confined fuselage sections, and wreck penetration into the interior should only be attempted with appropriate training — it is not a risk-free activity regardless of how open the structure appears.

## Conditions

- **Best season**: Year-round; May–October for best visibility
- **Visibility**: 15–25 m typical
- **Current**: Very mild in lagoon setting
- **Water temperature**: 27–28°C
- **Entry**: Boat dive; site marked by buoy

## Tips

A torch is useful for exploring the cockpit and fuselage interior. Operators in Papeete and Punaauia run this site as part of combination wreck dives including the adjacent schooner wreck. Given the confirmed depth range extends to approximately 25 m, this site is better suited to Intermediate-level divers than absolute beginners, though the shallower fuselage sections are accessible to newer Open Water divers under guide supervision. Wreck penetration of the interior compartments requires appropriate training and equipment — confined spaces with overhead environments should not be entered without proper preparation, and penetration is never risk-free regardless of how open a wreck's structure looks.

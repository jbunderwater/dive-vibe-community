## HMCS Annapolis

HMCS Annapolis is a former Royal Canadian Navy Annapolis-class destroyer, scuttled as an artificial reef in Halkett Bay Marine Provincial Park, off Gambier Island in Howe Sound, on 4 April 2015. She sits upright on the seabed with her upper structure within recreational depth.

## Overview

HMCS Annapolis (DDH 265) was laid down on 2 September 1961, launched on 27 April 1963 and commissioned in December 1964. She served on the East Coast until August 1989 and then on the West Coast, and went into extended readiness in December 1996. After the ship was turned over to Crown Assets in 1998, a 1999 attempt to save her as a floating museum failed. The Artificial Reef Society of British Columbia (ARSBC) took possession on 1 April 2008 and scuttled her on 4 April 2015 after legal disputes over environmental concerns. She sank in under two minutes. DAN and the ARSBC's project list describe her as the society's eighth reef project.

Sources differ on some details:

- **Length**: 366 ft (111.6 m) on Wikipedia and DAN; 371 ft (113 m) in X-Ray Mag and Hakai Magazine.
- **Depth**: Wikipedia and Hakai Magazine give a seabed depth of 32 m with the tops of the stacks at 10.5 m. The ARSBC gives low-tide figures of 105 ft at the bow and 98 ft at the stern, with the stack tops at 35 ft, the bow top at 60 ft, the flight deck at 68 ft and the stern top at 77 ft. X-Ray Mag and DAN give about 110 ft.
- **Displacement**: Wikipedia lists 3,420 long tons full load in its infobox and 2,400 tonnes initially in its text.
- **Decommissioning**: Wikipedia's infobox gives 15 November 1996, while its text gives a pay-off on 1 July 1998.

X-Ray Mag describes large holes in the hull, an intact helicopter hangar and covered walkways along both sides, and notes that penetration training is required before entering the wreck. It also says several dives are needed to see the whole ship.

## Site Information

- **Location**: Halkett Bay Marine Provincial Park, off Gambier Island, Howe Sound; DAN describes about a 20-minute boat ride from Horseshoe Bay
- **Entry Type**: Boat dive
- **Site Type**: Wreck dive
- **Difficulty Level**: Advanced
- **Maximum Depth**: 32 meters (sources report 32-33.5 m)
- **Best Time**: No site-specific season was found

## Marine Life

The wreck has been the subject of an ongoing biodiversity study (the Annapolis Biodiversity Index Study, run with the Vancouver Aquarium and citizen-science divers). Hakai Magazine reported about 80 species of flora and fauna after 14 dives, and the ARSBC reported nearly 50 species by early 2016 and a project list of over 170 species by June 2021.

Reports for this wreck name:

- Anemones, barnacles, gobies, shiner perch, hydroids, tubeworms and bryozoans soon after sinking, followed by spot prawns, coonstripe shrimp, purple and mottled sea stars, rockfish, pollock, greenlings and sculpins (X-Ray Mag).
- Rockfish, with four rockfish species and juveniles recorded over five years (ARSBC), and chitons, limpets and snails grazing the diatom mat (ARSBC).
- Lingcod, including two successful egg masses recorded in the year before June 2021 (ARSBC).

## Dive Profile

DAN reports numerous hull and deck openings and three buoyed ascent and descent lines, with much of the structure between about 50 and 80 ft. X-Ray Mag describes areas shallower than about 18 m suitable for less experienced divers, with the deeper bow and stern near 30 m.

## Entry and Exit

Access is by boat from Vancouver-area operators; DAN describes a boat ride of about 20 minutes from Horseshoe Bay. Use the buoyed lines and a surface marker buoy.

## Tips and Recommendations

- The ARSBC asks divers not to scrape or mark the hull, because that damages the diatom mat that supports the local food web.
- Plan several dives if you want to see the whole ship (X-Ray Mag).
- Cold water means thermal protection is required.

## Safety Considerations

The deeper parts of the wreck sit near 30 m in cold water, so this site is rated Advanced overall. X-Ray Mag states that divers need penetration training before entering; entering any wreck carries risk even with large openings. Dive within your training and use a surface marker buoy.

## Photography

Hakai Magazine and the ARSBC describe encrusting growth on the hull, which is the main subject for macro photography.

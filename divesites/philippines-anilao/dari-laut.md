# Dari Laut

The wreck of a former floating casino resting on a sandy slope, its open box-frame structure now heavily encrusted with sponges, gorgonia fans, featherstars, and anemones — an unusual dive with strong critter appeal.

## Overview

Dari Laut (the name combines "Dari," the reported name of the casino's owner, and "Laut," meaning sea in Malay/Indonesian) is considered the primary — and by several accounts the only real — wreck dive in the Anilao area. The structure is the remains of a floating casino/hotel-restaurant, described by different sources as either a purpose-built vessel or a converted barge. Sources disagree on when it operated: most describe it as a 1980s floating casino, while at least two independent sources (puertoparrot.com and scubaspotadvisor.com) date it to the 1970s. Multiple sources confirm it caught fire and sank; local speculation, repeated across several sources, holds that the owners deliberately burned it to collect insurance after poor revenue, but no source confirms this as fact rather than rumor. No verified dates of construction, dimensions, tonnage, or a Wikipedia-documented sinking record exist — these details remain unknown, and none of the "1970s vs 1980s" sources cite primary documentation for their dating.

The wreck's box-frame construction distinguishes it from traditional ship wrecks: the frame sits tilted on a sandy slope. Sources report slightly different depths for the frame — michaelmcfadyenscuba.info gives roughly 12–13 meters at the shallow end and 25 meters at the deepest, while puertoparrot.com gives 13 meters to 28 meters; one older source cites a maximum around 37 meters, which appears to be an outlier compared to the more detailed McFadyen and puertoparrot accounts and is not used here. Given the range across sources, this listing uses 12–28 meters for the frame. The open framework has been colonized by sponges, gorgonia fans, featherstars, and anemones, creating a productive critter habitat quite unlike a typical hull wreck.

## Site Information

- **Location**: Between Caban Island and Maricaban Island, Anilao, Batangas, Philippines (approximately 20 minutes west of Buceo Anilao Dive Resort)
- **Entry Type**: Boat dive
- **Site Type**: Wreck dive
- **Difficulty Level**: Intermediate
- **Maximum Depth**: 28 meters
- **Typical Visibility**: 12–22 meters
- **Water Temperature**: 29–30°C
- **Current**: None to mild
- **Best Time**: November to June (dry season)

## Wreck History

The Dari Laut was a floating casino/hotel-restaurant. Sources conflict on when it operated: most (including michaelmcfadyenscuba.info-era accounts and Lines of Latitudes) describe the 1980s, while puertoparrot.com and scubaspotadvisor.com independently describe the 1970s. Multiple sources confirm it sank after catching fire; the cause of the fire itself is disputed — persistent local speculation, repeated on several dive-related pages, holds that the owners deliberately burned it for insurance after the venture failed to meet revenue targets, but no source confirms this as established fact rather than rumor. No verified vessel dimensions, tonnage, or precise sinking date have been found in any source, and no Wikipedia article exists for the wreck. Divers and researchers should treat any specific dates or dimensions found on unofficial sources with caution, as the wreck's history is poorly and inconsistently documented.

## Marine Life

The encrusted box-frame supports a diverse community of marine life. Batfish school near and around the structure. Clownfish (including tomato clownfish, per michaelmcfadyenscuba.info) occupy anemones that have colonized the frame. Crocodilefish lie camouflaged on flat horizontal surfaces. Moray eels inhabit the many recesses of the framework. Nudibranchs and flatworms are found across the sponge-covered surfaces in considerable variety — michaelmcfadyenscuba.info specifically records striate chromodoris, Kunie's chromodoris, and a black-speckled elysia sea slug. Electric clams have also been observed on the structure. Puertoparrot.com additionally reports mandarin fish and crab-eye gobies at the site. The gorgonia fans and featherstars provide habitat for associated invertebrates throughout the frame.

## Dive Profile

Enter by back roll or giant stride and descend to the wreck. The shallowest part of the frame is at 12–13 meters, allowing extended bottom time. Explore around and through the open box-frame, examining the encrusted surfaces at each depth zone. The deepest frame sections reach 25–28 meters; the sandy bottom lies at approximately 30 meters. The open construction allows natural light to penetrate throughout the structure. Work from deep to shallow to manage no-decompression limits efficiently.

## Entry and Exit

Access by boat from Anilao-area resorts, approximately 15–20 minutes depending on departure point. Deploy an SMB during your safety stop for boat pickup.

## Tips and Recommendations

- The open box-frame structure is well-lit and easy to navigate — less intimidating than enclosed wreck penetration
- Check the anemones for clownfish and the flat surfaces for crocodilefish
- The shallowest sections at 12–13 meters make for an excellent extended safety stop area
- Bring a torch to illuminate the sponge-covered framework and bring out colours

## Safety Considerations

The box-frame structure is open, but be aware of potential entanglement points when carrying cameras or trailing equipment. Buoyancy control is important to avoid disturbing silt on the sandy slope beneath the frame. The deepest sand at 30 meters limits no-decompression time on air — plan accordingly. As with any wreck, do not attempt penetration of enclosed spaces without appropriate training and equipment.

## Photography

The Dari Laut offers both wide-angle and macro opportunities. The open box-frame silhouetted against blue water with dense marine growth is a distinctive composition unlike most wreck photographs. Batfish schooling around the structure suit wide-angle setups. Macro targets include nudibranchs on the sponges, clownfish in anemones, crocodilefish on flat surfaces, and electric clams. A wide-angle lens captures the wreck's unusual character, but a macro lens rewards careful exploration of the encrusted surfaces.

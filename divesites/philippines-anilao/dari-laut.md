# Dari Laut

The wreck of a former floating casino resting on a sandy slope, its open box-frame structure now heavily encrusted with sponges, gorgonia fans, featherstars, and anemones — an unusual dive with strong critter appeal.

## Overview

Dari Laut (the name combines "Dari," the reported name of the casino's owner, and "Laut," meaning sea in Malay/Indonesian) is considered the primary wreck dive in the Anilao area. The structure is the remains of a floating hotel-casino-restaurant. Sources disagree on the decade it operated and sank: one source (Puerto Parrot) places the sinking in the 1970s, while another (PaparazSea) describes it as "a former floating casino in the late 70's or early 80s." Four independent sources (Michael McFadyen, Puerto Parrot, PaparazSea, Scuba Diver Life) agree it was a floating casino/hotel-restaurant structure, and multiple sources (Puerto Parrot, McFadyen, PaparazSea) confirm it caught fire and sank. Two independent sources (McFadyen, PaparazSea) separately report a persistent local account that the owners deliberately burned the vessel to collect insurance after it failed to generate sufficient revenue — both sources frame this explicitly as local rumor/allegation, not confirmed fact. No verified vessel name, dates of construction, dimensions, or tonnage have been found in any source — these details remain unknown.

The wreck's box-frame construction distinguishes it from traditional ship wrecks: the frame sits tilted on a sandy slope. Sources disagree somewhat on exact depths: McFadyen (a diver's field report) puts the frame between roughly 12 and 25 meters; Puerto Parrot cites a maximum depth of 37 meters; other secondary sources round to a 12–13 meter shallow end and 25–28 meter deep end with a ~30 meter sandy bottom. Given McFadyen's account is a first-hand dive log, this description treats 12–25m as the most reliable frame range, while noting some sources report depths as deep as 37 meters. The open framework has been colonized by sponges, gorgonia fans, featherstars, and anemones, creating a productive critter habitat quite unlike a typical hull wreck.

## Site Information

- **Location**: Between Caban Island and Maricaban Island, Anilao, Batangas, Philippines (approximately 20 minutes west of Buceo Anilao Dive Resort)
- **Entry Type**: Boat dive
- **Site Type**: Wreck dive
- **Difficulty Level**: Intermediate
- **Maximum Depth**: 28 meters
- **Typical Visibility**: 12–22 meters
- **Water Temperature**: 29–30°C
- **Current**: None to mild
- **Best Time**: November to June (dry season)

## Wreck History

The Dari Laut was a floating hotel-casino-restaurant. Sources disagree on the exact decade — Puerto Parrot places the sinking in the 1970s, while PaparazSea describes it as "late 70's or early 80s." Multiple independent sources confirm it sank after catching fire. Two independent sources (McFadyen, PaparazSea) report — but explicitly frame as local rumor, not confirmed fact — that the owners deliberately set the fire to collect insurance after the venture failed to generate sufficient revenue; PaparazSea calls this "typical story for failed investments" rather than a documented account. No vessel name, construction date, dimensions, or tonnage have been verified in any source. Divers and researchers should treat any specific dates, names, or dimensions found on unofficial sources with caution, as the wreck's history is not well documented and no primary maritime record has been located.

## Marine Life

The encrusted box-frame supports a diverse community of marine life. Batfish school near and around the structure. Clownfish occupy anemones that have colonized the frame. Crocodilefish lie camouflaged on flat horizontal surfaces. Moray eels inhabit the many recesses of the framework. Nudibranchs and flatworms are found across the sponge-covered surfaces in considerable variety. Electric clams have also been observed on the structure. Puerto Parrot additionally reports the wreck is "now home to Mandarin fishes and Crab Eye Gobies" — both confirmed by that source as resident at this specific site. The gorgonia fans and featherstars provide habitat for associated invertebrates throughout the frame. Note: one operator page (anilaodiving.com) describes the site instead as a deliberately-sunk artificial reef of "a sunken floating barge, concrete blocks, and other man-made objects," with a generic reef-fish list (snappers, groupers, sea turtles, reef sharks) not corroborated by any other source and not used here — the floating-casino/fire narrative is the well-corroborated account.

## Dive Profile

Enter by back roll or giant stride and descend to the wreck. The shallowest part of the frame is at 12–13 meters, allowing extended bottom time. Explore around and through the open box-frame, examining the encrusted surfaces at each depth zone. The deepest frame sections reach 25–28 meters; the sandy bottom lies at approximately 30 meters. The open construction allows natural light to penetrate throughout the structure. Work from deep to shallow to manage no-decompression limits efficiently.

## Entry and Exit

Access by boat from Anilao-area resorts, approximately 15–20 minutes depending on departure point. Deploy an SMB during your safety stop for boat pickup.

## Tips and Recommendations

- The open box-frame structure is well-lit and easy to navigate — less intimidating than enclosed wreck penetration
- Check the anemones for clownfish and the flat surfaces for crocodilefish
- The shallowest sections at 12–13 meters make for an excellent extended safety stop area
- Bring a torch to illuminate the sponge-covered framework and bring out colours

## Safety Considerations

The box-frame structure is open, but be aware of potential entanglement points when carrying cameras or trailing equipment. Buoyancy control is important to avoid disturbing silt on the sandy slope beneath the frame. The deepest sand at 30 meters limits no-decompression time on air — plan accordingly. As with any wreck, do not attempt penetration of enclosed spaces without appropriate training and equipment.

## Photography

The Dari Laut offers both wide-angle and macro opportunities. The open box-frame silhouetted against blue water with dense marine growth is a distinctive composition unlike most wreck photographs. Batfish schooling around the structure suit wide-angle setups. Macro targets include nudibranchs on the sponges, clownfish in anemones, crocodilefish on flat surfaces, and electric clams. A wide-angle lens captures the wreck's unusual character, but a macro lens rewards careful exploration of the encrusted surfaces.

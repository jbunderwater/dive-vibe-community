## WIT Shoal II Wreck

The WIT Shoal II is a large steel hulk resting upright on a sand plain off St. Thomas — not the "210-foot hopper dredge" described in an earlier version of this page.

## Overview

Naval records (Wikipedia, corroborated by navsource.org) identify the wreck as USS LST-467, a Landing Ship, Tank built by Kaiser Shipyards in Vancouver, Washington, and commissioned in 1943 for WWII service in the Asiatic-Pacific theater. After the war she was decommissioned, sold into civilian hands, and converted for use as a log hauler before eventually being disposed of in Virgin Islands waters. Sources disagree sharply on when and how she came to rest at her current site: naval disposal records cite a 1979 sinking as an artificial reef, while several St. Thomas dive-tourism sites describe a 1984 sinking during Tropical Storm/Hurricane Klaus — either as a storm-driven grounding of a cement-carrying freighter, or as the failure of a hull patch while the ship was under tow for scrapping. This listing does not resolve that conflict; readers should treat the exact sinking date and circumstances as unconfirmed. Length is also inconsistently reported: local dive sites commonly cite 400 feet, while the LST-1 class she was built to typically ran closer to 328 feet — the true figure for this specific hull is not independently confirmed here. Depth is likewise reported inconsistently, with two sources placing the wreck around 27 meters (90 feet) and an earlier version of this page citing 21 meters (70 feet); this update adopts 27 meters based on the two most detailed sources found, but readers should be aware sources range from roughly 21-27 meters.

## Site Information

- **Location**: US Virgin Islands, Caribbean
- **Entry Type**: Boat dive
- **Site Type**: Wreck dive
- **Difficulty Level**: Intermediate
- **Maximum Depth**: 27 meters

## Marine Life

The wreck is heavily colonized with orange cup coral and rises roughly 21 meters (70 feet) from keel to wheelhouse across five decks. Sources report groupers, sharks, stingrays, and barracuda around the structure. Penetration of the interior spaces is possible for trained wreck divers, but always carries risk and should not be attempted without proper training and equipment.

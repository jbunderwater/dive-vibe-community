# Wildcat

A rare aircraft wreck in the Rade de Bormes — a Royal Navy Grumman F4F Wildcat (Martlet V) resting upside down at 51–54 metres with genuinely disputed origins.

## Overview

The Wildcat is one of the most unusual dive sites in the Hyères archipelago: a single-seat carrier-based fighter identified by Royal Navy cockades (English roundels) still visible on the fuselage as a Grumman F4F Wildcat — designated the "Martlet V" in British Fleet Air Arm service. The aircraft rests inverted on a sandy bottom at 51–54 metres (sources vary within this range), with its broken nose, open cockpit, intact propeller, and machine guns in the wings still recognisable. The engine is missing. It is a technically demanding dive due to depth and the frequently strong current at the site.

**The circumstances that placed the aircraft on the seabed are genuinely disputed, and two independently sourced, mutually incompatible accounts exist. Neither is presented here as fact.**

- **1950s jettison theory** — the majority view among dive-specific and official sources (destination.portcros-parcnational.fr, the official Port-Cros National Park dive-site page; aquabormes.fr; tribloo.com). This account holds that the aircraft possibly served post-war aboard the French aircraft carrier Hermès, became irreparably damaged, and was deliberately jettisoned at sea — tentatively dated to the 1950s (tribloo.com) or specifically 1956 (aquabormes.fr), though no source treats the date as confirmed. The retracted landing gear is cited as consistent with a deliberate ditching rather than a crash landing.
- **1944 combat-loss theory** — a single, more specific account from cirkwi.com, citing US archival records: a Wildcat V of 882 Naval Air Squadron, flying from the escort carrier HMS Searcher during Operation Dragoon (the Allied landings in Provence), crashed on a reconnaissance mission on 15 August 1944, with its pilot recovered. HMS Searcher and 882 Squadron did operate in the Provence landings, which lends some historical plausibility, but no dive-operator or official park source corroborates this specific wreck being that aircraft.

A previous version of this description stated the 1944 claim was "incorrectly attributed" to bormesplongee.fr and had no real source — that was true of the mis-attribution, but a fresh search found the claim does have a genuine, if singular, source (cirkwi.com) that this description had not previously checked. Readers should treat the aircraft's fate as unresolved rather than settled in either direction.

The aircraft's wingspan is reported as approximately 11.6 metres (sources round to "over 11 m" or "12 m") and its length as approximately 8.8 metres (sources round to "over 8 m" or "9 m"), consistent with the historical F4F Wildcat/Martlet V. It carried a 1,200 hp Pratt & Whitney engine (now absent from the wreck). Armament is confirmed by the official park source and by tribloo.com as 4 machine guns (12.7 mm) plus provision for two 45 kg bombs — a widely repeated "6 machine guns total" figure could not be traced to any primary source consulted and has been dropped. The Royal Navy operated more than 1,200 F4F Wildcats under the Martlet designation during World War II.

## Site Information

- **Location**: Rade de Bormes, near Île de la Fourmigue, Var, France
- **Entry Type**: Boat dive
- **Site Type**: Wreck dive
- **Difficulty Level**: Advanced
- **Maximum Depth**: 54 meters
- **Typical Visibility**: 10–25 metres
- **Water Temperature**: 13–25°C
- **Current**: Often strong; check conditions before diving
- **Best Time**: June to September (best current windows)

## Marine Life

Sources disagree on how much life the site actually supports. The official park page and tribloo.com describe it as sparse — tube worms (spirographs), scorpionfish, and red scorpionfish, with tribloo.com explicitly noting "nothing exceptional" and only "a few conger eels and mullet" patrolling the wreck. Other operator pages (aquabormes.fr, miopalmoplongee.com) additionally mention galatheas (squat lobsters) sheltering near the propeller and occasional lobsters in fuselage openings. Taken together, this is a low-biomass site rather than a marine-life highlight — no source attributes groupers, swallowtail sea perch, or gorgonian colonisation to this specific wreck, so regional Mediterranean species should not be assumed present here.

## Dive Profile

Descent directly on a line from the boat to the inverted aircraft at 51–54 metres. The fuselage shape is identifiable from above. Explore the accessible exterior: wings, tail assembly with propeller, fuselage. The cockpit is open but the interior is not accessible. Bottom time on air at this depth is severely limited — approximately 10–12 minutes to a no-decompression limit; Nitrox 32 extends this to approximately 15 minutes. Plan the deepest point first. Begin ascent with substantial reserve.

## Entry and Exit

Boat access from operators in La Londe or Le Lavandou who cover the Hyères deep wreck sites. Descent line is mandatory — do not free-descend in open water at this depth. Giant stride entry; descend directly and efficiently. Ascend on the line; deploy SMB from the 5-metre safety stop before surfacing.

## Tips

- Confirm current conditions before every entry — the site is known for strong and variable current
- Brief the dive plan in detail: fuselage orientation, depth of key features, ascent timing
- Nitrox 32 or 36% is strongly recommended to make safe use of limited no-decompression time
- Finding a single inverted aircraft on open sand at depth is a completely different experience from a ship wreck — the isolation is striking

## Safety

Advanced or technical certification required. Strong current can make ascent without a line extremely dangerous at this depth. Carry two SMBs. Gas planning must be conservative given depth, current, and complexity. Always dive with an operator who knows the current patterns at this site. Wreck penetration is not appropriate here.

## Photography

The inverted aircraft is a distinctive and unusual wide-angle subject. Shooting along the wing line with a diver for scale conveys the fuselage shape clearly. The open cockpit and machine gun detail reward closer macro work. Full artificial lighting is essential at this depth — no ambient colour remains.

## транспортный рефриджератор "Петрозаводск"

The Petrozavodsk refrigerator transport is a modern shipwreck dive site at Bear Island (Bjørnøya), Svalbard.

## Overview

The MS Petrozavodsk was a Russian refrigerated cargo vessel (a "reefer" acting as a freezer/support ship for Barents Sea fishing fleets) that ran aground on the southern tip of Bear Island (Bjørnøya), Norway, on 11 May 2009. She was launched on 4 September 1978 by Skala Skipasmidja in the Faroe Islands under the name Nordlandia, entered service in February 1980, and was later renamed Zapolyarye (2003) and Petrozavodsk (2004). The vessel measured 67 metres in length with a beam of 12 metres and a gross tonnage of 1,130 GT; sources give differing deadweight figures (1,287 DWT vs. 1,372 DWT), so that specific number is uncertain. All 12 crew were rescued after the ship struck rocks at the base of the island's cliffs, tearing gashes in her port side.

Norwegian courts later sentenced the captain to 15 days and the first officer to 40 days in prison after both were found to have been under the influence of alcohol; the officer was additionally found to have fallen asleep on duty while the vessel entered a protected zone off Bear Island's southern coast (closed to vessels over 40 feet between 15 May and 31 August to protect nesting seabirds).

The wreck remained in place because salvage was judged too dangerous due to rockfall risk from the surrounding cliffs. By 2011 the vessel had split into two sections, complicating removal further. A diesel spill was reported at the time; sources differ on the exact volume, but multiple reports describe it as a sheen that dissipated/evaporated rather than a major, lasting spill. The main environmental concern cited by Norwegian authorities was the grounding's location within an internationally significant seabird breeding area. The wreck has continued to disintegrate in the Arctic environment since.

The coordinates place this wreck near Bear Island (Bjørnøya), which lies between Svalbard and mainland Norway at approximately 74°N. Diving here is extreme: Arctic water temperatures, potential sea ice, remote location, and deep decompression obligations make this accessible only to highly experienced technical or polar dive expeditions. The site has not been documented as a recreational dive destination by established polar dive operators.

## Wreck History

- **Built**: launched 4 September 1978 by Skala Skipasmidja, Faroe Islands, as Nordlandia; entered service February 1980
- **Name history**: Nordlandia → Zapolyarye (2003) → Petrozavodsk (2004)
- **Type**: Refrigerated cargo vessel (reefer), used as a freezer/support ship for the Russian fishing fleet
- **Dimensions**: 67 m length, 12 m beam, 1,130 GT gross tonnage; deadweight reported as 1,287-1,372 DWT depending on source
- **Wrecked**: 11 May 2009, southern tip of Bear Island (Bjørnøya)
- **Cause**: Ran aground on rocks; captain and first officer later sentenced (15 and 40 days respectively) after being found intoxicated, officer also cited for falling asleep on duty in a protected zone; all 12 crew rescued
- **Current state**: Split into two sections by 2011; disintegrating in situ

## Dive Profile

- **Entry**: Boat (extremely remote; Bear Island has no permanent dive operations)
- **Maximum depth**: ~25 m
- **Water temperature**: Below 0°C to ~4°C year-round
- **Conditions**: Arctic; sea ice possible; extreme remoteness; rescue response near-impossible
- **Required experience**: Technical/polar diving experience only

## Safety

This site is in one of the world's most hostile diving environments. Bear Island has no permanent human infrastructure for diving support. Sea ice, sub-zero water temperatures, extreme remoteness, and the physical condition of the disintegrating wreck make this site unsuitable for recreational diving. Any dive operation here would require full expedition logistics including surface support vessel, decompression chamber, and polar diving experience. This entry is documented for completeness; the site is not recommended for recreational divers.

# Ray of Hope Nassau

A 200-foot freighter, last legally registered in Haiti, deliberately sunk as an artificial reef off Nassau in 2003, resting upright at 12–18 metres in clear Bahamian water and one of the most accessible wreck dives in the archipelago.

## Overview

The Ray of Hope is a 200-foot freighter deliberately sunk as an artificial reef off the southwest coast of New Providence Island, Nassau, on 7 July 2003. Nassau port authorities donated the vessel to Stuart Cove's Dive Bahamas for sinking — it became the 16th named wreck on the southwestern side of New Providence. According to Stuart Cove's own account, the vessel's documented history is layered: she was constructed in Germany, held a prior registration in Belize, and was last legally registered as a Haitian freighter before being sunk. The ship sits upright with its bow at approximately 12 metres (40 ft) and the stern sloping down to 18 metres (60 ft), making this one of the most accessible and diver-friendly wrecks in Nassau. The wreck is fully intact, allowing divers to explore its interior, cabins, and gangways, with the large cargo hold open and crossed with beams that cast shadows across the floor. It sits directly behind an older wreck called Bahama Mama, creating a two-wreck dive option. Caribbean reef sharks regularly gather around the wreck, and it is used for structured shark-feeding dives by Stuart Cove's.

## Marine Life

Multiple sources agree the wreck's marquee attraction is Caribbean reef sharks, which gather here in numbers for Stuart Cove's shark-encounter dives; nurse sharks and eagle rays are also reported. Angelfish, snapper, grunts, grouper, and horse-eye jacks are reported around the structure, along with corals, sponges, and anemones colonising the hull. Sea turtles are occasional visitors. Earlier claims of resident "glassfish and sweepers," spotted moray eels, and southern stingrays on a nearby 50-meter sand bottom were not corroborated by the sources reviewed and have been removed.

## Dive Profile

Descent to the bow at approximately 12 metres. The wreck slopes gently toward the stern at 18 metres. The accessible depth allows generous bottom times — a complete circuit of the wreck takes 30–45 minutes. Interior exploration of the hold, cabins, and gangways is possible from multiple deck hatches. The adjacent Bahama Mama wreck can be included in a single dive for experienced divers.

## Entry and Exit

Boat dive from Nassau-based operators, primarily Stuart Cove's Dive Bahamas. Entry by backward roll or giant stride. Permanent mooring on the wreck.

## Tips and Recommendations

The accessible depth (12–18 m) makes this one of Nassau's most diver-friendly wrecks, suitable for newly certified divers. A torch helps in the interior sections — the hold and cabins have interesting structure but reduced natural light. Stuart Cove's runs structured shark-feeding dives at this site if the shark encounter experience is a priority; follow the operator's briefing closely, as feeding dives require specific positioning and behavior.

## Safety Considerations

Interior sections should not be penetrated beyond natural light without wreck diving training. Silt disturbance in the interior can reduce visibility rapidly. The site is operated by Stuart Cove's for shark-feeding dives — follow divemaster briefings closely for this component, and do not attempt to feed or touch sharks independently.

## Photography

The accessible depth (12–18 m) means good natural light — strobes are optional for exterior shots. Wide-angle shots of the superstructure with a diver descending provide scale. Interior shots through portholes and deck openings use the classic "window frame" wreck photography composition. The shark encounters at this site provide additional photographic opportunities.

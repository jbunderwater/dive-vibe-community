# Curtiss C-46A-45-CU Commando

A twin-engine cargo aircraft resting just below the surface off Norman's Cay, shallow enough to snorkel — one of the Bahamas' most unusual wreck dives, with a documented history connected to the island's notorious 1980s drug-trafficking era.

## Overview

The Curtiss C-46A Commando (civil registration N355BY, operated by BWI Leasing) crashed into the shallow waters off Norman's Cay on 15 November 1980 and came to rest on the sandy bottom just off the runway. Multiple independent accident records (Aviation Safety Network, Bureau of Aircraft Accidents Archives, Wikipedia) agree on the mechanics of the crash: during touch-and-go training, the aircraft touched down short, the pilot attempted a go-around, clipped an earthen berm tearing the left landing gear loose and the no.1 propeller into the runway, then banked left and came down in the sea. There were no fatalities. Norman's Cay was, between approximately 1978 and 1982, the base of operations for Carlos Lehder's cocaine trafficking network, and the island's own tourism material markets this as "Pablo Escobar's plane." Sources disagree on whether the aircraft itself was carrying cargo at the moment of the crash: some tourist-facing accounts describe it as "thought to be carrying cocaine," while a local historical account explicitly calls the overloaded-with-drugs version an invented legend, and the primary aviation accident records make no mention of cargo at all, describing only a training-flight mishap. What is well documented is the wreck's presence within Lehder's operating era at Norman's Cay, not the specific circumstances of this flight. The wreck sits remarkably intact — the fuselage, wings, and engine nacelles are all identifiable. Depth is misleadingly listed elsewhere as a deep dive; in reality the wreck sits in only about a metre or two of water and portions of the airframe break the surface at low tide, making it a popular snorkel site as much as a dive site. Norman's Cay's crystalline water delivers excellent visibility. Water temperature is 25–28°C seasonally.

## Marine Life

Sources describe the wreck generically as having become an artificial reef with "a rainbow of different fish," but none name specific species observed at this particular site. Given the destination's regional fauna, small reef fish and invertebrate colonisation on the airframe are likely, but no site-specific marine life reports were found — treat any species list for this wreck as unconfirmed.

## Dive Profile

Because the wreck sits in only 1–4 metres of water, this is closer to a snorkel-with-fins profile than a conventional wreck dive — divers and snorkellers alike can examine the cockpit, fuselage, and wing sections from just below or at the surface. Bottom time is effectively unlimited at this depth; the limiting factor is boat scheduling rather than air supply.

## Entry and Exit

Boat dive/snorkel accessed via Norman's Cay or Exuma-based operators, or liveaboard. Norman's Cay is remote — reaching this site typically requires an overnight stay on Norman's Cay or a liveaboard itinerary that includes the Exuma Cays. Entry by giant stride or roll-in from a shallow-draft tender. The very shallow depth means exit and navigation are straightforward.

## Tips and Recommendations

Because portions of the wreck can break the surface at low tide, time your visit around the tide table if photographing the airframe fully submerged is the goal. The aircraft's wings provide a strong wide-angle composition element. This is a unique subject among Bahamas dive sites — an aircraft wreck is a very different experience from the ship wrecks found elsewhere in the archipelago, and its shallow depth makes it accessible to snorkellers as well as certified divers.

## Safety Considerations

Even at this shallow depth, the aircraft interior should not be penetrated without wreck diving training — sharp edges and unstable panels are a real hazard in a four-decade-old wreck. The remote Exuma/Norman's Cay location means emergency support is further away than at Nassau-area sites. Watch for boat traffic given how close the site sits to the surface.

## Photography

An aircraft wreck in crystal-clear, very shallow Bahamas water is an exceptional photography subject — natural light is abundant at 1–4 metres and strobes are largely unnecessary. Wide-angle captures the full aircraft silhouette against the sandy bottom. Close-focus wide-angle on the cockpit nose section is a classic image.

# Curtiss C-46A-45-CU Commando

A shallow, half-submerged twin-engine cargo aircraft resting off the southern end of Norman's Cay — one of the Bahamas' most unusual snorkel-and-dive sites, with a documented history connected to the island's notorious 1980s drug-trafficking era.

## Overview

The Curtiss C-46A Commando (civil registration N355BY, construction number 30243) crashed into the shallow waters off the southern end of Norman's Cay on 15 November 1980 and came to rest on a sandbar between two outcrops of land. Aviation-accident records (Bureau of Aircraft Accidents Archives and Aviation Safety Network) describe the accident as occurring during touch-and-go training: the aircraft touched down short, the pilot attempted a go-around, clipped an earthen berm tearing the left landing gear loose, and the aircraft came down in the sea just off the runway. There were no fatalities among the one crew member and one passenger aboard. Popular tourism accounts add colorful, unverified embellishments — a supposedly intoxicated pilot, cargo hastily rescued before authorities arrived — but these details are not corroborated by the aviation-accident-specific sources and are not repeated here. Norman's Cay was, between approximately 1978 and 1982, the base of operations for Carlos Lehder's cocaine trafficking network — the wreck is historically associated with that period, though the documented cause of the crash itself was pilot error during a training flight, not a smuggling incident. The C-46 Commando design dates to the early-to-mid 1940s. The wreck rests in very shallow water — sources consistently describe it as roughly 3 meters (10 feet) deep depending on tide, with sections of the fuselage breaking the surface at low tide, making it accessible to snorkelers as well as divers. Visibility in the surrounding water is typically good, though exact figures were not found in the sources reviewed.

## Marine Life

Multiple sources describe the wreck as having been colonised into an artificial reef that attracts "a rainbow of different fish and marine life," but none of the sources reviewed name specific species sighted at this site. Rather than repeat an unconfirmed species list, this section is left general: divers and snorkelers can expect typical shallow Exuma Cays reef and sand-flat fauna, but no site-specific marine-life reports were found for this wreck.

## Dive Profile

Because the wreck sits in only 2–4 meters of water, this is effectively a single shallow pass rather than a staged descent. The cockpit, fuselage, and wing sections can each be examined from the surface or with minimal descent. A complete circuit of the aircraft is quick, leaving ample time for photography; many visitors combine it with snorkeling rather than scuba.

## Entry and Exit

Boat/day-tour access via Norman's Cay or Exuma-based operators from Nassau, or by private/charter boat. Entry by giant stride or simply sliding in from a small tender given the shallow depth. The sandy surroundings make for easy, low-risk exit.

## Tips and Recommendations

The aircraft's wings provide a good wide-angle composition element — approach from the nose end for the classic front-of-aircraft shot. This is a unique subject among Bahamas wreck sites — an aircraft wreck is a very different experience from the ship wrecks found elsewhere in the archipelago, and its shallow depth makes it one of the few "wreck dives" in this dataset that beginners and snorkelers can enjoy without technical wreck training.

## Safety Considerations

Do not penetrate the fuselage interior; the structure has been deteriorating for over four decades and is not stable for entry. Boat traffic near the wreck can be present given its popularity as a day-tour stop — maintain awareness of surface traffic. Norman's Cay is remote, so emergency support is farther away than at Nassau-area sites.

## Photography

The wreck's shallow depth and generally clear water make it a strong wide-angle subject even for snorkelers, with natural light reaching the aircraft at all times of day. Close-focus wide-angle on the cockpit nose section is a classic composition. The encrusted propellers and engine nacelles work well as closer-in subjects.

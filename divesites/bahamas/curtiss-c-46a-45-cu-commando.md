# Curtiss C-46A-45-CU Commando

A remarkably intact twin-engine cargo aircraft resting in the shallows off Norman's Cay — one of the Bahamas' most unusual wreck dives, with a documented history connected to the island's notorious 1980s drug-trafficking era.

## Overview

The Curtiss C-46A Commando (civil registration N355BY) crashed off Norman's Cay on 15 November 1980. The aviation-accident record (Bureau of Aircraft Accidents Archives and Aviation Safety Network) attributes the crash to touch-and-go training: the aircraft touched down short, the pilot attempted a go-around, clipped an earthen berm tearing the left landing gear loose, and the aircraft came down in the shallows just off the runway. There were no fatalities. Norman's Cay was, between approximately 1978 and 1982, the base of operations for Carlos Lehder's cocaine-trafficking network, and popular travel accounts sometimes describe the pilot as intoxicated and the aircraft as actively smuggling cargo at the time of the crash. A first-hand account published by a local operator instead describes the pilot ("British Andy") having previously flown for the Lehder organization and having offered the aircraft to Lehder for possible purchase, but states the plane was not carrying contraband on the accident flight — the official accident databases likewise record the cause as a training-flight mishap, not a smuggling run. Given these conflicting accounts, the exact circumstances beyond the touch-and-go mechanics (and whether the pilot was impaired) are not independently confirmed. The twin-engine C-46 Commando design dates to the 1940s and was widely used postwar for Caribbean freight operations. Contrary to some published depth figures, the wreck actually rests in very shallow water — reported at around 3 metres (roughly 10 feet), with a section of the airframe breaking the surface at low tide — making it a popular snorkel site as much as a dive site. The fuselage, wings, and engine nacelles remain identifiable despite four decades of coral and sponge colonisation. Norman's Cay's crystalline water delivers excellent visibility.

## Marine Life

No site-specific marine-life reports were found for this wreck. Given its shallow depth and four decades of reef colonisation, small reef fish and some coral/sponge growth on the airframe are likely, consistent with regional Exuma Cays fauna, but no source confirms particular species at this site — claims about resident groupers, moray eels, or turtles here should be treated as unconfirmed until a site-specific report is found.

## Dive Profile

Because the wreck sits in only 1–4 metres of water, this is a shallow, unhurried exploration rather than a multi-level dive. Divers (and snorkelers) can circle the fuselage, wings, and engine nacelles at the surface or on a single breath/short dive. A complete exterior circuit takes 15–20 minutes; total time in the water is flexible since there is no depth-driven no-decompression concern.

## Entry and Exit

Boat dive/snorkel accessed via Norman's Cay or Exuma-based operators, or liveaboard. Norman's Cay is remote — this site is typically visited as part of an overnight stay or liveaboard itinerary in the Exuma Cays. Entry by giant stride or simply sliding in from a shallow-draft boat given the depth. Exit is straightforward given the shallow, sandy surroundings.

## Tips and Recommendations

Because part of the airframe can break the surface at low tide, check tide timing before visiting — the wreck is visible at low tide but fully submerged at high tide per official wreck records. A mask and snorkel alone can reveal much of the site; a dive is not required to appreciate it. This is a unique subject among Bahamas dive sites — an aircraft wreck is a very different experience from the ship wrecks found elsewhere in the archipelago.

## Safety Considerations

The aircraft interior should not be penetrated further than arm's length without wreck diving training. Even in shallow water, sharp and corroded metal edges are a laceration hazard — gloves and caution around the airframe are recommended. The remote Exuma location means emergency support is further away than Nassau sites.

## Photography

An aircraft wreck in crystal-clear, shallow Bahamas water is an exceptional photography subject, well suited to natural light given the shallow depth. Wide-angle captures the full aircraft silhouette against the sandy bottom. Close-focus wide-angle on the cockpit nose section is a classic image.

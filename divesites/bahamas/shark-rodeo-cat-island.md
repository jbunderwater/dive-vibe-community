# Shark Rodeo Cat Island

Cat Island's famous oceanic whitetip shark dive — one of the few places in the world where oceanic whitetip sharks (Carcharhinus longimanus) reliably approach divers at attainable depths, in an open-water "blue water" drift-diving format that operators describe as requiring advanced buoyancy skills (see Overview and Dive Profile).

## Overview

The Shark Rodeo at Cat Island is one of the most significant shark diving experiences in the Bahamas — primarily because of the species involved. Oceanic whitetip sharks (Carcharhinus longimanus) were once among the most abundant large sharks in the open ocean but have been devastated by longline fishing globally; multiple operators describe Cat Island as one of the last reliable places on Earth to encounter them. **This re-audit found a significant mischaracterization in the previous pass: this is not a shallow, stationary, sandy-bottom dive.** It is an open-water "blue water" drift dive — divers enter open ocean off Cat Island's eastern shelf edge, near where the Atlantic meets the Antilles Current, with no fixed bottom in sight. Epic Diving describes it as "drifting over bottomless ocean"; Southern Boating & Yachting independently calls it an "open-ocean drift dive [requiring] advanced buoyancy skills, unlike the shallow stationary dives at other Bahamian locations"; X-Ray Mag reports divers' only point of reference is a bait crate suspended at roughly 10 metres, with the sharks themselves capable of diving to around 1,000 m. Southern Boating separately describes Cat Island's wall system beginning around 12 m (40 ft) at its shallowest and reaching roughly 914 m (3,000 ft) across more than 20 miles of wall; Scubaverse independently reports the dive site itself is about a 10-minute boat ride from shore. No single source gives one precise "bottom depth" for the shark encounter, since there is no bottom in view during the dive — the 12 m figure in this record should be read as an operating/reference depth, not a seafloor depth.

Sources also disagree on the exact season, more than the previous text acknowledged: Epic Diving's own site states May–June; X-Ray Mag reports "end of March to mid-June"; Southern Boating and Scubaverse describe a broader window extending from March/April through June/July; and multiple sources agree that whitetip numbers peak around April–May, coinciding with the tuna migration. The species is not present here year-round. The primary operator is Epic Diving, run by Vincent and Debra Canabal aboard the M/V Thresher — based at **Greenwood Beach Resort**, not Hawk's Nest Resort as the previous version of this page stated (see Entry and Exit below for the source of that correction).

## Marine Life

Oceanic whitetip sharks are the primary attraction during the site's season (see Overview for the range of season estimates reported by different sources) — identifiable by their distinctive rounded, mottled white-tipped fins and pectoral fins broad as wings. Four independently re-verified sources (Epic Diving, Southern Boating, Scubaverse, Big Fish Expeditions) confirm oceanic whitetips specifically at this site, and two (Epic Diving, Big Fish Expeditions) additionally confirm silky sharks and dusky sharks here — species omitted from the previous version of this page despite being well-sourced. Epic Diving describes the whitetips as staying close to the surface during the encounter itself; however, this describes the shallow drift-dive encounter specifically, not a general species trait — Scuba Diver Life documents oceanic whitetips elsewhere as powerful divers capable of descending to roughly 1,000 m when hunting migrating prey, so the previous text's blanket claim that the species is "generally slow-moving" has been softened here as unsupported at that generality.

**Caribbean reef sharks — corrected:** The claim that reef sharks are "present year-round and typically encountered on every dive" is not supported by any source checked today and has been removed. Big Fish Expeditions states that Caribbean reef sharks, lemon sharks, nurse sharks, and even tiger sharks "sometimes make an appearance" at the whitetip site — an occasional, not year-round, presence. Separately, research on Hawk's Nest Marina — a different Cat Island location on the island's southern tip where sport fishers clean their catch — found that nurse sharks, lemon sharks, Caribbean reef sharks, and spotted eagle rays reliably gather there for the fish discards. That is a distinct site and a distinct (chum/discard-based) shark experience from the open-water oceanic whitetip drift dive, and the previous version of this page appears to have conflated the two. The claim that "the shallow, sandy bottom supports nurse sharks and southern stingrays" has been removed for the same reason: there is no sandy bottom at the whitetip drift-dive site (see Overview), and the ray/shark species in question were only confirmed at Hawk's Nest Marina, not here.

## Dive Profile

This is an open-water "blue water" drift dive, not a stationary sand-bottom dive as the previous version of this page described. Divers enter open ocean with no fixed bottom in sight; X-Ray Mag reports that operators suspend a bait crate at roughly 10 m as the only visual reference point, and diving is "very much a three-dimensional experience" as a result. Oceanic whitetips are inquisitive and, per Big Fish Expeditions, "usually arrive within a few minutes" of chumming and "generally stick around all day" once present — sometimes in groups of six to ten simultaneously per one source. Divemasters/crew manage the encounter and shark approaches from the boat or via the reference line. Southern Boating notes this style of diving requires advanced buoyancy skills and is explicitly distinguished by operators from the shallow, stationary dives typical of other Bahamian shark sites (e.g. Tiger Beach). Divers should expect sharks to approach closely and occasionally bump into them as part of investigative behavior (X-Ray Mag).

## Entry and Exit

Boat dive from Cat Island. The primary operator is Epic Diving, which runs the M/V Thresher out of **Greenwood Beach Resort** — confirmed independently by Epic Diving's own site, Scubaverse, and Big Fish Expeditions (which separately lists "Dive Shop at Greenwood Beach Resort" as an operator). The previous version of this page named Hawk's Nest Resort as Epic Diving's base; that appears to be an error — Hawk's Nest Resort & Marina is a real, separate property on Cat Island's southern tip, associated with a different fish-discard shark encounter (see Marine Life), not with the oceanic whitetip drift dive. Scubaverse independently reports the whitetip dive site is roughly a 10-minute boat ride from Greenwood Beach Resort. Cat Island (New Bight Airport) is reached from Nassau by scheduled flight; flight-schedule data indicates roughly 30–45 minutes' flight time (Bahamasair's scheduled route is commonly listed around 40 minutes), correcting the previous "approximately 1 hour" estimate, which this audit could not confirm from any dive-related source. Entry by giant stride. DSMB required for surface pickup.

## Tips and Recommendations

Oceanic whitetips behave differently from Caribbean reef sharks — they are more persistent and investigative, consistent with Big Fish Expeditions' description of sharks arriving quickly and remaining around the boat for extended periods. Maintain calm positioning and avoid sudden movements. Do not extend hands toward approaching sharks. This is an open-ocean blue-water drift dive at very few comparable sites worldwide, and — per Southern Boating and X-Ray Mag — requires advanced buoyancy and open-water comfort beyond what a stationary reef or sand-flat shark dive (e.g. Nassau's shark feeds, or Tiger Beach) demands; it should not be treated as an easier or more casual dive than those sites.

## Safety Considerations

Oceanic whitetips are the species most historically associated with open-ocean shipwreck survivor attacks. Their behavior toward divers in the drift-dive format used at Cat Island is generally described by operators as manageable with an experienced crew and strict adherence to briefings — but this is not a shallow, controlled "recreational setting" as the previous version of this page characterized it; it is an open-ocean encounter with a pelagic predator and should not be described as safe. Strictly follow all operator briefings. Do not wear high-contrast black-and-yellow dive gear, a commonly cited (though not site-specific) general shark-diving precaution. DSMB mandatory.

## Photography

Oceanic whitetip photography is the primary goal. The broad pectoral fins and rounded nose create a distinctive silhouette — position at the same depth as the shark, not above it, for the best full-body images. The crystal-clear Bahamas water supports excellent natural-light photography, though — per the Dive Profile above — there is no sand or reef backdrop; images are typically shot against open blue water rather than a white-sand background as the previous version of this page described.

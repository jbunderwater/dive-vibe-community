# Shark Rodeo Cat Island

Cat Island's celebrated oceanic whitetip shark encounter — a blue-water drift dive over open ocean, one of the few places in the world where oceanic whitetip sharks (Carcharhinus longimanus) reliably approach divers.

**Naming note:** Our research turned up no source that uses the name "Shark Rodeo" for this Cat Island oceanic whitetip site. "Shark Rodeo" is a well-documented, distinctly different named dive site at Walker Cay in the Abaco Islands, where up to 100 blacktip and Caribbean reef sharks gather around a baited "chumsicle." The Cat Island oceanic whitetip experience described below is real and well sourced, but every source we found refers to it as "oceanic whitetip diving/shark diving at Cat Island," not "Shark Rodeo." This looks like a naming mix-up in our data — flagging for review rather than renaming, since that's outside this audit's scope.

## Overview

Cat Island's offshore waters are one of the last reliable places on Earth to encounter oceanic whitetip sharks (Carcharhinus longimanus), a species once among the most abundant large sharks in the open ocean but devastated globally by longline fishing. This is not a reef dive: multiple independent sources describe it as an open-ocean, blue-water drift dive with no seabed reference — divers drift over water that can be "eighty to ninety feet or thousands of feet deep," in one operator's words, on the edge of Cat Island's steep drop into the Exuma Sound / Atlantic. Sources disagree on the peak season, citing windows anywhere from March through July, with April–June cited most often as when oceanic whitetips follow the tuna migration through the area. Caribbean reef sharks, silky sharks, and dusky sharks are also reported at this site, with reef sharks, nurse sharks, and lemon sharks more likely when drifting closer to shore.

## Marine Life

Oceanic whitetip sharks are the primary draw, identifiable by their distinctive rounded, white-tipped dorsal and pectoral fins. Silky sharks and dusky sharks are reported alongside them by multiple independent sources (Southern Boating, Big Fish Expeditions). Caribbean reef sharks, nurse sharks, and lemon sharks are reported as occasional visitors, more common when the drift carries divers closer to shore. One source (Southern Boating) additionally lists blacktip, tiger, hammerhead, and blue sharks as possible — we could only find this claim in one place, so treat it as unconfirmed rather than a reliable expectation. Occasional blue marlin, mahi-mahi, and tuna are reported passing through.

## Dive Profile

This is a blue-water drift dive, not a stationary bottom dive. Divers descend into open water and drift with the boat/current rather than settling on a seabed — there typically isn't one within recreational range. Oceanic whitetips are inquisitive and often approach within the first half hour. Good buoyancy control is essential since divers can't use the bottom to stabilize themselves.

## Entry and Exit

Boat dive — day-boat or liveaboard, operated by Cat Island—based specialists (Epic Diving, based near Hawk's Nest) and expedition operators (Big Fish Expeditions) running multi-day trips, often via liveaboard catamaran. Cat Island is reachable by roughly a 1-hour flight from Nassau. Entry by giant stride into open water. DSMB required for surface pickup given the blue-water, no-fixed-exit-point nature of the dive.

## Tips and Recommendations

Oceanic whitetips are more persistent and investigative than Caribbean reef sharks and will return repeatedly to check divers out. Maintain calm positioning and avoid sudden movements; do not extend hands toward approaching sharks. This is a genuinely different experience from Nassau's shallow reef-shark encounters — there's no bottom, no cage, and no feeding station, just open ocean and a wild pelagic species.

## Safety Considerations

Oceanic whitetips have a well-documented history of interactions with shipwreck survivors in open ocean, though guided recreational encounters with experienced operators are managed carefully. Strictly follow operator briefings. Because this is a no-bottom blue-water dive, maintaining group contact and carrying a DSMB are essential — there is no reef or wall to orient by if you get separated.

## Photography

Oceanic whitetip photography is the main goal. The broad pectoral fins and rounded nose create a distinctive silhouette — position at the same depth as the shark, not above it, for full-body shots. Clear Bahamas water supports natural-light photography without strobes at the depths typically worked. Wide-angle or fisheye is standard, with open blue water as the background rather than reef or sand.

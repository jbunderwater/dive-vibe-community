# Shark Rodeo Cat Island

Cat Island's famous oceanic whitetip shark dive — one of the few places in the world where oceanic whitetip sharks (Carcharhinus longimanus) reliably approach divers at recreational diving depths.

## Overview

The Shark Rodeo at Cat Island is one of the most significant shark diving experiences in the Bahamas — primarily because of the species involved. Oceanic whitetip sharks (Carcharhinus longimanus) were once among the most abundant large sharks in the open ocean but have been devastated by longline fishing globally; the species is now assessed as critically endangered. Cat Island's offshore waters remain one of the last reliable places on Earth where divers can encounter them predictably. The oceanic whitetips arrive at Cat Island in the largest numbers in April and May, following the tuna migration through the area — operators describe the season as centered on April–May with sightings possible from as early as March into June (one operator's expedition dates run into early June, another into July). Caribbean reef sharks are present throughout the year. This is **not** a shallow sandy-bottom sit-and-wait dive: divers drift in open blue water directly over the drop-off near the island's continental shelf edge, where the bottom falls away to roughly 1,000 metres within a short distance of shore — there is no sand bottom beneath divers. The sharks themselves tend to stay near the surface, so diving depth is shallow (a few meters down to roughly 8–12 m) even though the water column below is, in practice, bottomless. The clear Bahamian water delivers 25–35 metres visibility in typical conditions.

## Marine Life

Oceanic whitetip sharks are the primary attraction, peaking April–May — identifiable by their distinctive rounded, white-tipped fins and pectoral fins broad as wings. These are genuinely pelagic animals; their seasonal concentration at Cat Island is exceptional and follows the annual tuna migration. Caribbean reef sharks are present year-round and are commonly encountered on the same trips. Multiple operators describe the whitetips as curious and investigative — approaching divers directly rather than being purely "slow-moving," though they do tend to stay near the surface. Operators also report occasional silky, dusky, blue, and tiger sharks, and great hammerheads in Cat Island's waters more broadly, though these are not a reliable feature of every Shark Rodeo dive specifically. No source-confirmed claims of resident nurse sharks or stingrays "on the bottom" apply here, since there is no bottom at diving depth.

## Dive Profile

This is an advanced, cageless, open-water drift dive: divers enter and hold position in the blue at roughly 4–12 meters over water that drops away to great depth beneath them — there is no bottom to settle on. Oceanic whitetips are inquisitive and will typically approach closely within minutes of entry, circling divers from all directions. Divemasters position themselves to manage shark approaches. Bottom time is generous given the shallow depth, but the open-water, no-reference-point setting itself is what makes this an advanced-level dive.

## Entry and Exit

Boat dive from Cat Island operators — primarily Epic Diving (based at Hawk's Nest Resort & Marina), which specialises in oceanic whitetip expeditions, or via liveaboard visiting the island. Cat Island is reached from Nassau by a short flight — sources put this at roughly 30 minutes by small aircraft, considerably shorter than earlier estimates of an hour. Entry by giant stride. DSMB required for surface pickup.

## Tips and Recommendations

Oceanic whitetips behave differently from Caribbean reef sharks — they are more persistent and investigative, and will return repeatedly to investigate divers. Maintain calm positioning and avoid sudden movements. Do not extend hands toward approaching sharks. The experience of a true pelagic species at recreational diving depth is unique to very few sites worldwide — Cat Island is a pilgrimage destination for shark divers who have already experienced Nassau and Tiger Beach. Because this is a blue-water dive with no reef or sand reference below, it is recommended for divers who are comfortable in open water with no bottom in sight; an advanced certification is required by operators.

## Safety Considerations

Oceanic whitetips are the species most historically associated with open-ocean shipwreck-survivor attacks; their behaviour with experienced divemasters managing a controlled, cageless encounter is generally described by operators as manageable, but this is inherently a higher-risk dive than a reef encounter given the species and the open-water setting. Strictly follow all operator briefings. Do not wear high-contrast black-and-yellow dive gear, which can attract shark attention. DSMB mandatory.

## Photography

Oceanic whitetip photography is the primary goal. The broad pectoral fins and rounded nose create a distinctive silhouette — position at the same depth as the shark, not above it, for the best full-body images. The crystal-clear Bahamas water makes natural-light photography excellent at this shallow depth. Wide-angle or fisheye captures the full shark with the white sand background.

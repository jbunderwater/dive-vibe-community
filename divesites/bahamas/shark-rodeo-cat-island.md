# Shark Rodeo Cat Island

Cat Island's famous oceanic whitetip shark dive — one of the few places in the world where oceanic whitetip sharks (Carcharhinus longimanus) reliably approach divers at recreational diving depths.

## Overview

**Correction (2026-07-13 re-audit):** "Shark Rodeo" is a real, documented Bahamas dive-site name — but it belongs to a different location and a different kind of dive: the Caribbean reef/blacktip shark feeding site at Walker Cay, in the Abacos (far north Bahamas), where up to ~100 sharks gather at a "chumsicle" bait can. No source found uses "Shark Rodeo" to describe the Cat Island oceanic whitetip encounter. This entry's coordinates (24.3833, -75.4333) are correctly placed at Cat Island, and the oceanic-whitetip content is well sourced (see footer), but the "Shark Rodeo" name itself appears to be a misattribution/conflation with the unrelated Walker Cay site and should be corrected or removed by whoever maintains site naming. Content below is limited to what is actually sourced for Cat Island's oceanic whitetip diving.

Cat Island's offshore waters are one of the last reliable places on Earth to encounter oceanic whitetip sharks (Carcharhinus longimanus) — a species once among the most abundant large sharks in the open ocean, now severely depleted globally by longline fishing. Multiple independent sources confirm the sharks gather here each spring, drawn by a tuna migration; peak numbers are reported in April–May, with the broader season running roughly late March through mid-June depending on the source. This is **not** a feeding dive in the Stuart Cove sense — operators report the sharks stay around without active feeding required, and the encounters take place as open-water/blue-water drift dives near the island's shelf edge, where the bottom drops toward nearly 1,000 metres within a short distance of shore, rather than as a stationary sit-on-the-sand encounter. A bait box is typically used as a draw point at a shallow working depth (one source specifies roughly 10 m), but this is a drifting blue-water dive, not a bottom sit. The clear Bahamian water delivers strong visibility in typical spring conditions.

## Marine Life

Oceanic whitetip sharks are the confirmed primary attraction, peaking April–May as they follow migrating tuna — identifiable by their large, rounded, white-tipped fins. Multiple sources (Epic Diving, Big Fish Expeditions) also confirm silky sharks and dusky sharks as commonly encountered on the same blue-water dives, and note Caribbean reef sharks, lemon sharks, nurse sharks, and occasional tiger sharks are seen when drifting closer to shore. No site-specific source confirms southern stingrays at this location — that claim has been removed rather than presented as confirmed. Sharks here are described by multiple sources as inquisitive and willing to approach divers closely, occasionally making physical contact out of investigative curiosity rather than aggression.

## Dive Profile

This is an open-water/blue-water drift dive, not a stationary sand-bottom sit like Tiger Beach. Divers enter the water over deep, open ocean near the shelf edge and drift at a shallow working depth (commonly cited around 10 m, with the operable range roughly 4–12 m). Oceanic whitetips are inquisitive and often approach within minutes. Divemasters manage shark approaches, and dives can run long given the shallow depth and the sharks' persistence in staying near the group. Because there is no bottom or wall reference, divers should have solid buoyancy control and comfort with open-water/blue-water diving.

## Entry and Exit

Boat dive from Cat Island operators — primarily Epic Diving (based at Hawk's Nest Resort) which specialises in the oceanic whitetip expeditions, or via liveaboard visiting the island. Cat Island requires flights from Nassau to New Bight Airport (TBI), typically 30–45 minutes by small aircraft. Entry by giant stride. DSMB required for surface pickup.

## Tips and Recommendations

Oceanic whitetips behave differently from Caribbean reef sharks — they are more persistent and investigative, and will return repeatedly to investigate divers. Maintain calm positioning and avoid sudden movements. Do not extend hands toward approaching sharks. The experience of a true pelagic species at recreational diving depth is unique to very few sites worldwide — Cat Island is a pilgrimage destination for shark divers who have already experienced Nassau and Tiger Beach.

## Safety Considerations

Oceanic whitetips are the species most historically associated with open-ocean shipwreck attacks, though their behaviour in a shallow recreational setting with experienced divemasters is generally manageable. Strictly follow all operator briefings. Do not wear high-contrast black-and-yellow dive gear, which can attract shark attention. DSMB mandatory.

## Photography

Oceanic whitetip photography is the primary goal. The broad pectoral fins and rounded nose create a distinctive silhouette — position at the same depth as the shark, not above it, for the best full-body images. The crystal-clear Bahamas water makes natural-light photography excellent at this shallow working depth. Because this is a blue-water drift dive rather than a sand-flat encounter, expect open-blue backgrounds rather than a white-sand backdrop; wide-angle or fisheye still captures the full shark well.

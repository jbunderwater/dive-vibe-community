# S.S. Sapona

A Prohibition-era concrete freighter lying half-submerged off Bimini — a Bahamian landmark that has been a destination for divers and snorkellers since the 1930s.

## Overview

The S.S. Sapona is one of the most unusual and historically evocative wrecks in the Bahamas. Launched in October 1919 by the Liberty Shipbuilding Company in Wilmington, North Carolina, the Sapona was built as a concrete-hulled cargo steamer for the United States Shipping Board's WWI Emergency Fleet — but the armistice came first and she never served in wartime (a small number of tourism sites give an earlier launch date, but this is not corroborated by the ship's documented build program and is treated here as unreliable). After changing hands several times, she was purchased in 1924 by Carl G. Fisher, the developer of Miami Beach, for just $4,000, and subsequently acquired by Bruce Bethell, a former British officer who moved her to Bimini to serve as a floating warehouse for his Prohibition-era liquor operation. She broke free and drove aground at Barnett Harbour during the Great Miami Hurricane of 1926; salvage was deemed too costly and she was abandoned where she grounded. During World War II, the wreck served as a target for U.S. Army Air Force and Navy training exercises — strafed with machine-gun fire and bombed repeatedly. On 5 December 1945, Flight 19 — five U.S. Navy Avenger torpedo bombers — flew a training bombing run on the Sapona before disappearing on the return leg, after which target practice on the wreck ceased. Today the roughly 282-foot (281 ft 10 in) concrete hull lies partially above the waterline; sources report a range of about 4.5–6 metres (15–20 ft) of water at the wreck. It remains as much a surface landmark as an underwater dive site, and is visible and diveable year-round.

## Marine Life

Dive and snorkel reports from the wreck describe a lively reef community on and around the hull. Sergeant majors and moray eels shelter on the structure, alongside schools of grunts (bluestriped and French) and blackbar soldierfish. Nurse sharks are frequently reported resting under the hull near the stern. Other species reported at the site include French angelfish, great barracuda, spotfin butterflyfish, schoolmaster snapper, bar jack, triggerfish, grouper, and lobsters sheltering in the wreck's crevices. Encrusting sun coral covers sections of the exposed hull. (Earlier drafts of this listing named parrotfish, blue tang, Southern stingrays, and Green sea turtles at this site, plus fire coral colonisation; no site-specific source could be found to confirm those, so they have been removed.)

## Dive Profile

Boat to the wreck, entry by giant stride or ladder into the shallow water. Divers swim around the submerged sections of the hull at 2–5 meters. The wreck is large enough to spend a full dive exploring — the hull sections have multiple swimming angles and interesting structural features. Surface intervals can be spent viewing the above-water sections of the hull. A complete exploration of the underwater portions takes 30–45 minutes.

## Entry and Exit

Boat dive from Bimini-based operators. The wreck is close to the main islands of Bimini. Entry by giant stride from the boat. Exit to the boat via ladder or assistance.

## Tips and Recommendations

The Sapona is excellent as both a scuba dive and snorkel — the very shallow depth means the underwater sections are visible from the surface. Historical context (the Prohibition-era liquor warehouse story, the WWII bombing practice connection, and the Flight 19 proximity) significantly enhances the experience — read about the wreck's history before visiting. The hull is encrusted with coral growth and has sharp, weathered concrete edges — maintain awareness and avoid contact. The above-water sections of the wreck make for dramatic photos from the surface.

## Safety Considerations

The Sapona's concrete surface is encrusted with coral growth and worn by decades of weathering and historic bombing damage — avoid contact with the hull. The exposed position of the wreck means Atlantic swell can create surge on the hull structure in rough conditions; currents can also pick up with the tide. Check conditions with operators before booking. The hull sections above water are structurally compromised — do not attempt to board the exposed sections, and do not attempt any wreck penetration without proper training, equipment, and a qualified guide.

## Photography

The partially above-water wreck creates unique photography opportunities. Split-level shots showing the underwater hull and the above-water wreck sections simultaneously are a signature image. The schools of sergeant majors on the concrete walls photograph well in ambient light at this shallow depth. Atmospheric wide-angle shots with the rust-stained concrete and marine colonisation create a visually compelling subject.

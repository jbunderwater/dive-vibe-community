# S.S. Sapona

A Prohibition-era concrete freighter lying half-submerged off Bimini — a Bahamian landmark that has been a destination for divers and snorkellers for decades.

## Overview

The S.S. Sapona is one of the most unusual and historically evocative wrecks in the Bahamas. Launched on 11 October 1919 by the Liberty Shipbuilding Company in Wilmington, North Carolina, the Sapona was built as a concrete-hulled cargo steamer under the United States Shipping Board's WWI Emergency Fleet program — a mass wartime shipbuilding effort undertaken because steel was scarce, not a vessel designed for or by any single individual. The armistice came before she saw wartime service. Note: several tourism sites repeat a popular but unsupported claim that the ship was personally commissioned by President Woodrow Wilson and "designed by Henry Ford" — Wikipedia's sourced account attributes her instead to the standard Shipping Board emergency-fleet concrete-ship program, and that is the version followed here. After the war, Miami Beach developer Carl G. Fisher purchased the vessel in 1924 for $4,000; per DreamWrecks and Bahamas.com, Fisher briefly used the hull as a casino and later for oil storage before selling it on. It subsequently passed to Bruce Bethell, a former British officer, who moved her to Bimini to serve as a floating warehouse for his Prohibition-era liquor operation. She was driven aground at Barnett Harbour during a hurricane in 1926 and abandoned — salvage was deemed too costly. During World War II, the wreck served as a target for U.S. Army and Navy bombing and strafing practice. Many tourism sources state that this target practice ceased after five U.S. Navy torpedo bombers (the "Flight 19" squadron) disappeared on 5 December 1945; this specific causal link is repeated widely in Bahamas tourism writing but is not corroborated by primary Flight 19 accounts, which document the squadron's bombing-practice run at Hens and Chickens Shoals rather than at the Sapona itself — the Sapona/Flight 19 connection should be treated as unconfirmed. Today the 281-foot 10-inch (2,795 GRT, 3,500 DWT) concrete hull lies partially above the waterline, as much a surface landmark as an underwater dive site, and is visible and diveable year-round.

## Marine Life

The Sapona's exposed concrete surfaces are colonised by a shallow-water reef community. Sergeant majors are consistently reported schooling around the wreck. Multiple sources also report angelfish, parrotfish, French grunt, bluestriped grunt, schoolmaster snapper, bar jacks, moray eels, and barracuda around the structure. Fire coral has colonised significant sections of the exterior.

## Dive Profile

Boat to the wreck, entry by giant stride or ladder into shallow water. Divers swim around the submerged sections of the hull at roughly 3–6 meters. The wreck is large enough to spend a full dive exploring — the hull sections have multiple swimming angles and interesting structural features, and sunlight through holes in the deck illuminates much of the interior. Surface intervals can be spent viewing the above-water sections of the hull. A complete exploration of the underwater portions takes 30–45 minutes.

## Entry and Exit

Boat dive from Bimini-based operators. The wreck is a short boat ride from the main islands of Bimini. Entry by giant stride from the boat. Exit to the boat via ladder or assistance.

## Tips and Recommendations

The Sapona works well as both a scuba dive and a snorkel outing — the shallow depth means the underwater sections are visible from the surface. Historical context (the Prohibition-era liquor warehouse story and the WWII bombing-practice use) enhances the experience — but treat the Henry Ford/Woodrow Wilson origin story and the Flight 19 connection as unverified popular lore rather than established fact. Fire coral is extensive on the hull surface — maintain awareness and avoid contact. There is also jagged exposed rebar throughout the structure; a current tetanus vaccination is a sensible precaution before visiting.

## Safety Considerations

Fire coral (Millepora) is abundant on the Sapona's concrete surface — avoid any contact with the hull. Jagged rebar is exposed in multiple places on the wreck; keep well clear to avoid cuts. The exposed position of the wreck means currents and Atlantic swell can be stronger than expected in rough conditions — check conditions with operators before booking, and do not dive or snorkel alone. The hull sections above water are structurally compromised — do not attempt to board the exposed sections.

## Photography

The partially above-water wreck creates unique photography opportunities. Split-level shots showing the underwater hull and the above-water wreck sections simultaneously are a signature image. The schools of sergeant majors on the concrete walls photograph well in ambient light at this shallow depth. Atmospheric wide-angle shots with the rust-stained concrete and marine colonisation create a visually compelling subject.

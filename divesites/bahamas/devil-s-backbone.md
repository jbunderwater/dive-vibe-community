## Devil's Backbone

The Devil's Backbone is a shallow, jagged reef extending across the northern tip of Eleuthera island — a site as notorious for wrecking ships as it is celebrated for the dive sites those wrecks have created.

## Overview

Stretching approximately 1.4 miles northeast of Spanish Wells at the north end of Eleuthera, the Devil's Backbone has been documented as a navigational hazard since 1648. More ships have wrecked here than on any other reef in the Bahamas. The result is a remarkable collection of shallow wrecks layered atop one another in 8-11 meters of water, all now heavily colonised by coral and sponge and hosting abundant marine life. The reef itself is an intricate system of coral heads, archways, and channels. The surf here is rarely calm, making the site effectively boat-access only despite the shallow depths.

## Notable Wrecks

The four wrecks below were cross-checked against multiple independent sources (Project Eleuthera, Dive the World's Eleuthera guide, Zentacle, Ocean Fox Bahamas, and The Tribune's historical feature on the reef). Vessel name, wreck year, approximate length, and general depth are corroborated by at least two independent sources for each wreck; a couple of additional details found in only one source are flagged below rather than stated as fact. No Wikipedia entry could be found for any of these four vessels, so none of this is Wikipedia-sourced.

- **The Cienfuegos** (wrecked 1895): A 292-foot American steam passenger ship that ran aground on the reef during a strong northwest gale in the early hours of February 4–5, 1895, roughly 5 miles north of Harbour Island. All passengers and crew survived; native seamen were credited with rescuing occupants of a capsized lifeboat, and some cargo (including rice) was salvaged. Sources agree the wreck now lies spread across roughly 3–10 metres of water, with the bow, steam engine, boiler, and driveshaft still identifiable — divers describe it as resembling an underwater junkyard. One source additionally states she was built in 1883 at Chester, Pennsylvania, weighed 2,332 tons, and was operated by the Ward Line — these specifics appear in only one source found during this review and are not independently confirmed, so treat them as unverified rather than established fact.
- **The Carnarvon** (wrecked 1916): A 186-foot steel-hulled Welsh freighter that serviced Bahamian lighthouses for the British Admiralty, and ran aground on a shallow coral head off North Eleuthera in 1916. Multiple sources place her in roughly 5–8 metres of water on the southern edge of the Backbone, northeast of Preacher's Cay, with the top of the steering quadrant about 2–3 metres below the surface. The steam engine and driveshaft are described as remarkably intact for a wreck over a century old, and divers can swim through part of the boiler.
- **The Train Wreck** (wrecked 1865): A barge carrying a steam locomotive and rail cars — reportedly Confederate-era equipment en route from the southern United States to a Cuban sugar plantation during the Civil War — that struck the reef in a storm and sank. Multiple sources place it in roughly 3–5 metres of water, with remaining artifacts including locomotive wheels, rail trucks, boiler plate, brass spikes, coal, ballast stones, and wooden structural remains.
- **The Vanaheim** (wrecked 1969): An 86-foot coastal freighter that ran aground during a storm in February 1969; her cargo of potatoes and onions gave the site its informal name "Potato and Onion Wreck." Multiple sources report her metal rudder lies only about five feet from the Cienfuegos's bow, since both vessels struck the reef in nearly the same spot. Sources put her depth at roughly 3–10 metres, consistent with the Cienfuegos alongside which she rests.

Divers should also be aware the Backbone holds wrecks beyond these four — Project Eleuthera documents an 1846 wreck (USS Boston, an 18-gun sloop of war) elsewhere on the reef, illustrating just how long this stretch of coral has been claiming ships. Its wreckage is not part of the four sites described above.

## Site Information

- **Location**: North Eleuthera, Bahamas (near Spanish Wells and Preacher's Cave)
- **Entry Type**: Boat dive
- **Site Type**: Wreck dive
- **Maximum Depth**: 8 meters
- **Typical Visibility**: 24-30 meters in the dry season (Nov-May); can drop significantly when surge or swell disturbs sand around the wrecks
- **Current**: Light to moderate
- **Best Time**: November to May (calmer conditions)

## Marine Life

General reef fish populations reported across the wrecks and reef include snapper, grunts, parrotfish, angelfish, Nassau grouper, sharks, stingrays, and moray eels, with brain coral forming part of the backdrop (per Ocean Fox Bahamas's site description). At the wreck level, the Cienfuegos specifically has been reported to host angelfish, parrotfish, and conch, while the Train Wreck's boiler plate and wheels attract snapper and nudibranchs. Earlier text on this page also named barracuda plus elkhorn and star coral specifically — these were not corroborated by site-specific sources during this review and have been removed rather than presented as confirmed.

## Dive Profile

The entire site sits within recreational depth limits, making it suitable for newly certified divers. Begin on the shallower sections and move between wrecks. The Train Wreck at 5 meters makes an ideal end to the dive during the safety stop zone. Multiple dive profiles are possible — the wrecks are spread across the reef and require several dives to cover fully.

## Entry and Exit

Boat dive. Ocean Fox Dive Shop on Harbour Island and other local operators offer guided wreck tours. The reef requires local knowledge to navigate safely due to its shallow, complex structure.

## Tips and Recommendations

- The Carnarvon wreck is particularly photogenic — wide-angle shots of the engine room work well
- The Train Wreck wheels and axles are unique subjects — found nowhere else in the Bahamas
- Book multiple dives to cover the spread of wrecks; a single dive will not do the site justice
- Check weather conditions — even moderate Atlantic swell makes the site difficult

## Safety Considerations

Boat traffic in the area can be significant — always use a dive flag. The shallow depths create surge in any swell, and sand or silt disturbed around the wreck structures can locally reduce visibility; maintain good buoyancy. The reef is fragile and the wrecks are over a century old in places — never characterize entering wreck interiors as safe; any interior exploration requires appropriate wreck-penetration training and should not be attempted casually. Do not penetrate unguided wreck interiors.

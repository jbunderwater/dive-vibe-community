# Willaurie Wreck Nassau

A 130-foot Bahamian mailboat that sank accidentally in 1988 and was resettled as an artificial reef off Nassau's southwest coast — an accessible wreck now colonised by sponges, corals, and large resident groupers.

## Overview

The Willaurie was a 130-foot (39.6 m) steel freighter built in the Netherlands in 1966, reportedly under the name *Willmary*, which traded in the UK before coming to the Bahamas. From 1980 she served as an inter-island mailboat under owner W.B. Hart of Nassau, transporting mail and freight between Nassau and the outer islands of Rum Cay, San Salvador, and Cat Island. Accounts of her final sinking differ in detail: some sources describe her breaking free of her tow lines during a storm while under way between New Providence's northern side and a Nassau marina, sinking near Clifton Pier by accident in 1988; a Bahamian maritime historian's account instead describes her as already under tow toward deliberate scuttling when the tow line parted and she grounded off Clifton Pier, and states the vessel sank multiple times during the salvage process before coming to rest. What all accounts agree on is that Stuart Cove's Dive Bahamas patched the hull at low tide, partially refloated the wreck, and moved it to its current resting position on the southwest side of the island, where she settled for good. The Willaurie now sits on the sandy bottom in roughly 15–21 metres of water (sources vary within this range). Its most distinctive feature is the open cargo hold — the metal support bars of the original mail compartment now form an underwater cage that soft corals have colonised extensively, especially around the propeller. The site has been a popular Nassau wreck for over three decades. Visibility is 20–30 metres. Water temperature 25–28°C.

## Marine Life

Site-specific reports describe grouper, snapper, angelfish, and blue tang around the structure, along with barracuda and sea turtles passing through. A spotted drum has been reported sheltering in the engine block area. Invasive lionfish have also been documented at the wreck, as they now are on most colonised Bahamian wrecks. Claims of resident "black grouper" specifically, spotted moray eels, or spiny lobster at this site are not confirmed by the sources reviewed and should be treated as generic padding rather than site-verified.

## Dive Profile

Descent via mooring line to the main deck at 10–12 meters. Exploration works bow to stern along the main deck before visiting the interior sections accessible from deck hatches. The deeper sections of the hull at 17–19 meters can be visited at the end of the dive before a safety stop at the upper deck. Total dive: 45–55 minutes.

## Entry and Exit

Boat dive with permanent mooring. Operated by Nassau-based dive operators. Entry by giant stride or backward roll. Exit is standard boat pickup after safety stop.

## Tips and Recommendations

The Willaurie is one of the best wreck dives in Nassau for beginner wreck divers — the accessible depth, upright position, and clear water make it a comfortable introduction to wreck exploration. Carry a torch for the interior sections where the groupers and moray eels shelter. The upper deck in 10–12 meters is well-lit by natural light and ideal for close-focus wide-angle photography.

## Safety Considerations

Interior sections should not be penetrated beyond light penetration without wreck diving training. Silt in the interior spaces can reduce visibility to near zero if disturbed. The accessible depth of 19 meters means no decompression concern for a standard dive duration. DSMB recommended for the ascent.

## Photography

The accessible depth and clear water make the Willaurie an excellent introduction to wreck photography. The upper deck in natural light at 10–12 meters allows wide-angle photography without strobes in the Bahamas' clear water. The resident groupers inside the superstructure are large and approachable. Wide-angle shots looking along the main deck from bow to stern capture the ship's scale.

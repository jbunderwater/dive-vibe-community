# Willaurie Wreck Nassau

A 130-foot Bahamian mailboat that sank accidentally in 1988 and was resettled as an artificial reef off Nassau's southwest coast — an accessible wreck now colonised by sponges, corals, and large resident groupers.

## Overview

Most dive-history sources agree the Willaurie was a 130-foot (39.6 m) steel freighter built in the Netherlands in 1966, which served the Bahamas from 1980 as an inter-island mailboat — transporting mail and freight between Nassau and the outer islands of Rum Cay, San Salvador, and Cat Island. One dive-site database instead lists a 1907 build in Denmark and a different early sinking history (at Potter's Cay, Nassau); that account is an outlier against the more detailed and mutually consistent mailboat history, so it is noted here rather than adopted. A local news account (Tribune242) corroborates that the Willaurie sank multiple times before its final loss: in 1988 the vessel broke free while under tow toward Clifton Pier / Sea Breeze Marina on New Providence's western shore and went down. On Christmas Day 1988, Stuart Cove's Dive Bahamas patched the hull at low tide, partially refloated the wreck, and attempted to move it — it took on water again and settled on its keel in its current position near Goulding Cay on the southwest side of the island, "a safe distance" from the Tongue of the Ocean drop-off. The Willaurie now sits upright on the sandy bottom in roughly 15–21 metres of water (sources vary within this range). Its most distinctive feature is the open cargo hold — the metal support bars of the original mail compartment now form an underwater cage that sponges and soft corals have colonised extensively, especially around the stern and propeller. A separate, older wooden-hulled wreck — reportedly a vessel that had carried Haitian refugees — lies scattered nearby off the starboard side. The site has been a popular Nassau wreck for over three decades. Visibility is 20–30 metres. Water temperature 25–28°C.

## Marine Life

A resident spotted drum is reported living around the engine block. The cargo-hold cage structure and the stern/propeller area carry dense, brightly coloured soft coral growth (reds, oranges, purples, and greens per diver reports), with sponges also colonising the metal framework. Fish move through the cargo bars, though sources do not name specific species beyond this general description — treat any more detailed fish list for this wreck as unconfirmed.

## Dive Profile

Descent via mooring line to the main deck in the mid-teens of metres. Exploration works bow to stern along the main deck and around the cargo-hold cage before visiting the interior sections accessible from deck hatches. The deepest hull sections (down toward 21 meters per some sources) can be visited at the end of the dive before a safety stop at the upper deck. Total dive: 45–55 minutes.

## Entry and Exit

Boat dive with permanent mooring. Operated by Nassau-based dive operators. Entry by giant stride or backward roll. Exit is standard boat pickup after safety stop.

## Tips and Recommendations

One source describes the Willaurie as offering "beginner to advanced" divers a good dive, and its upright, intact position makes it approachable for less experienced wreck divers despite depths reaching into the low 20s of metres. Carry a torch for the interior sections. The colourful coral growth on the cargo-hold cage is a highlight for photographers.

## Safety Considerations

Interior sections should not be penetrated beyond light penetration without wreck diving training. Silt in the interior spaces can reduce visibility to near zero if disturbed. The accessible depth of 19 meters means no decompression concern for a standard dive duration. DSMB recommended for the ascent.

## Photography

The clear water makes the Willaurie a strong wreck photography subject. The brightly coloured soft coral on the cargo-hold cage and stern/propeller area is the standout macro and close-focus wide-angle subject. Wide-angle shots looking along the main deck from bow to stern capture the ship's scale.

## SS President Coolidge

The SS President Coolidge is one of the world's great wreck dives — a 199-meter luxury liner converted to a WWII troopship, sunk by American mines in October 1942 off Espiritu Santo, Vanuatu, and diveable from recreational depth at the bow (21m) to technical depth at the stern (73m).

## Overview

Built at Newport News Shipbuilding and completed in 1931, the President Coolidge was a premier Pacific passenger liner of 21,936 gross tons before her wartime conversion to a troopship. On 26 October 1942, carrying roughly 5,000 American soldiers, she entered the mined Segond Channel at Luganville through the wrong lane and struck two mines. Her captain, Henry Nelson, ran her aground in an attempt to save the ship; she slipped off the reef shelf soon after and settled on her port side. Only two men died in the sinking — a fireman killed in the blast and an army captain, Elwood Euart, who died after going back inside to help evacuate men from the infirmary.

The wreck stretches roughly 200 meters along the seabed. The bow sits at about 21 meters; the stern reaches 73 meters. Because the ship rolled onto her port side, decks that were once horizontal are now vertical — a disorienting, distinctive feature of Coolidge diving. More than eighty years of marine growth have colonized the exterior in soft corals and sponges.

The Coolidge suits a range of diver levels. The upper superstructure and cargo areas are accessible to Open Water certified divers at recreational depths; the deeper cargo holds and engine room call for Advanced Open Water training or higher; the stern and deepest structural sections are technical-diving territory.

## What to Expect

No two Coolidge dives are the same — the ship is so large that a week of daily diving leaves significant areas unexplored. Key highlights include:

- **The Lady**: A porcelain relief panel known as "The Lady and the Unicorn," which once decorated the first-class smoking lounge. After the promenade deck partially collapsed, local divers recovered and re-installed the panel nearby. Sources give slightly different depths for it (roughly 33–39 meters) and even differ on the exact imagery — most describe a lady riding a unicorn, though at least one operator describes a lady on a horse. It remains the most famous single artifact on the wreck, and divers traditionally "kiss the lady" on the way past.
- **The Holds**: Multiple cargo holds contain recognizable military vehicles, field guns, jeeps, trailers, and supply crates.
- **Medical supplies and a barber's chair**: Bottles, syringes, and other medical equipment are visible in a B-deck hold, and a barber's chair sits in an alcove of another cargo hold.
- **The Guns**: Anti-aircraft gun mounts remain on the hull, now oriented sideways since the ship rolled onto her port side.

Most operators run the Coolidge as a shore dive — entry is from the beach near Luganville, close to Million Dollar Point, with a surface swim out to the descent line. Some operators use a boat for deeper technical dives or to save divers the surface swim. Local Ni-Vanuatu dive guides — who between them have logged tens of thousands of dives on the wreck — lead virtually all dives here, though this reflects standard operator practice rather than a documented legal mandate.

## Practical Information

- **Depth Range**: 21–73 meters (bow to stern; the accessible upper sections start around 20 meters)
- **Visibility**: Generally good year-round, though sources don't agree on precise numbers
- **Currents**: Generally calm at depth according to operator accounts
- **Entry**: Primarily a shore dive from near Luganville/Million Dollar Point; some operators also run it as a boat dive
- **Certification**: Open Water divers can access the upper superstructure and cargo areas; Advanced Open Water or higher is recommended for the deeper holds and engine room; technical certification is needed for the stern and deepest structural sections
- **Guides**: Local dive operators provide experienced guides for all dives, and penetration sections are led by guides as standard practice

## Marine Life Highlights

Confirmed site-specific sightings reported by dive operators include lionfish, triggerfish, nudibranchs, and a large resident moray eel nicknamed "Nessie" that has taken up residence near the bow winches. Soft corals and sponges have colonized much of the exterior hull.

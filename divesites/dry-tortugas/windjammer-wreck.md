## Windjammer Wreck

The Windjammer Wreck is the remains of the iron-hulled sailing ship *Avanti*, lost on Loggerhead Reef in 1907, resting in just 20 feet of water and encrusted with coral — one of the most accessible wreck dives in the United States.

## Overview

The Windjammer Wreck preserves the remains of the *Avanti*, a three-masted, iron-hulled sailing ship built in 1875 that struck Loggerhead Reef in the early morning of January 21, 1907, after a navigational error during a winter gale, and was recognized as a total loss the following day. She was carrying a lumber cargo from Pensacola to Montevideo, Uruguay at the time. All 19 crew survived. The vessel sank in approximately 20 feet (6–7 meters) of water about 1,100 yards southwest of Loggerhead Key, where its iron skeleton has remained largely intact due to iron's natural resistance to corrosion. The hull is now colonized by stony and gorgonian corals and populated by snapper, spiny lobster, and hogfish; a goliath grouper is possibly encountered hiding within the hull, per NPS. The NPS produces a laminated underwater trail guide available at the Garden Key visitor center.

## Wreck History

The vessel passed through three names. Built in 1875 as a three-masted, iron-hulled sailing ship, she was first registered as *Killean*. She was later sold to the French shipping company Antoine-Dominique Bordes & Fils and renamed *Antonin*, and finally sold to the Norwegian company Acties Avanti around 1901, becoming the *Avanti*. Her dimensions — 261.4 feet long, 39.3 feet beam, 17.5 feet draft, with a gross tonnage of 1,862 tons — are confirmed independently in both the NPS's dedicated Windjammer wreck pages and the NPS's 2003 interpretive brochure "Window to the Past: The Windjammer Wreck," a more detailed, dedicated historical document.

**Cause of sinking (confirmed):** While attempting to pass the Dry Tortugas in the early morning hours of January 21, 1907, the *Avanti* was caught in heavy winter winds and seas. A navigational error contributed to the disaster, and at 6:44 a.m. the ship struck Loggerhead Reef. The crew fought for hours to save the vessel — losing an anchor, rigging cable to the bitts, and eventually dropping a second anchor to keep her from sliding into deep water — but she took on water and broke apart, becoming a total loss recognized the next day, January 22, 1907. This account, drawn from the NPS's dedicated 2003 interpretive brochure, is independently corroborated by a separate source (Blue Turtle Cruising), which likewise attributes the wrecking to a navigational error. This supersedes an earlier, more cautious characterization of the cause as unrecorded — it is documented, not guessed at.

**Build location conflict (unresolved):** Sources disagree on where and by whom the ship was built. One NPS page states she was built by John Reid & Co. in Port Glasgow, Scotland; the NPS's own 2003 interpretive brochure states she was built in Liverpool, England, without naming a builder. Both sources agree on the 1875 build year. This is a genuine conflict between two NPS-published sources that this research could not resolve.

**Sale-to-Bordes date conflict (unresolved):** One NPS page states the ship was sold to Bordes and renamed *Antonin* in 1891. The NPS's 2003 brochure instead states the sale occurred in 1894, "after seven years" before the final 1901 sale to Acties Avanti — a timeline that is internally consistent with the brochure's own 1901 renaming date but not with the 1891 figure. Neither date could be confirmed as correct against an independent third source.

**Sinking date (largely confirmed, one outlier):** The January 21–22, 1907 date is corroborated by the NPS's dedicated brochure, two further NPS pages, and an independent web aggregation of sources. One secondary source (South Dade News Leader) instead cites January 9, 1907; given how consistently the Jan 21–22 date is corroborated elsewhere, that appears to be the outlier, but it has not been independently reconciled.

**Hull material:** The NPS's dedicated, detailed sources (the 2003 brochure and the in-depth wreck-history pages) consistently describe the hull as iron — a material choice the brochure explains was standard for large, durable sailing ships of that era. Two of the park's general visitor-information pages instead describe the hull as "steel." Because the dedicated historical documentation is more detailed and specific to this wreck, iron is treated as the better-supported figure here, but the discrepancy in the park's own general pages is noted for transparency.

All 19 crew aboard survived, and much of the lumber cargo was later salvaged. The wreck was discovered in 1971 by an archaeological survey crew and not positively identified as the *Avanti* until 1990, according to the NPS — this detail comes from NPS sources only and was not independently corroborated elsewhere in this research pass. An earlier claim that the wreck is listed on the National Register of Historic Places could not be confirmed against any source in this research pass and has been removed rather than repeated unverified.

## Marine Life

The NPS describes the Windjammer as "an aquarium of reef-dwelling, free-swimming, and benthic life." One NPS source notes a goliath grouper is "possibly" encountered hiding within the hull — worth watching for, but not guaranteed on every dive. Yellowtail and black snapper school around the gorgonian-encrusted framework, and the NPS's photograph captions identify hogfish and spiny lobster at the wreck. A separate source (Blue Turtle Cruising) confirms schools of colorful reef fish and barracuda at the site. Brain coral and stony corals have colonized portions of the hull. (Nurse sharks are documented elsewhere in the park but no source in this research confirmed them specifically at the Windjammer, so they are not listed here.)

## Dive Profile

The wreck lies approximately 1,100 yards southwest of Loggerhead Key in 18–21 feet (6–7 meters) of water. The hull has collapsed into two primary debris fields: a bow section approximately 110 feet long oriented east–west, and a roughly equal-length midships and stern section oriented north–south that retains masts and rigging. The wreck sits on a white sand bottom, providing excellent contrast and visibility. Visibility is typically excellent — the NPS describes it as suitable for snorkelers and divers together. A mooring buoy marks the site.

## Entry and Exit

Access is by boat only — the park is 70 miles west of Key West. The Windjammer is off Loggerhead Key, not Garden Key; visitors who arrive on the Yankee Freedom III ferry at Garden Key would need a separate vessel to reach the wreck. Private boats and dive charter vessels can anchor or use the mooring buoy on sandy bottom away from the wreck. The Yankee Freedom ferry cannot carry compressed dive tanks; divers must arrive via private or charter vessel equipped with tanks. A NPS mooring buoy marks the site — contact the NPS on VHF channel 16 for current mooring status.

## Tips and Recommendations

- Collect the NPS laminated underwater trail guide from the Garden Key visitor center before diving
- Spend time at both the bow and stern sections, which are oriented in different directions
- Bring a dive light to peer into recesses where lobster and grouper shelter
- Do not touch, move, or remove any artifacts — all shipwrecks and cultural resources within the national park are federally protected, and removal is a federal offense with serious penalties
- Lionfish have colonized Gulf reefs and wrecks; observe but do not handle them

## Safety Considerations

Maximum depth is approximately 7 meters, making depth-related narcosis and decompression non-issues. The park's remoteness demands self-sufficiency: no dive support infrastructure exists on the island, the nearest chamber is in Key West, and weather can deteriorate rapidly in the Gulf. Watch for fire coral growing on the wreck structure. Observe but do not handle lionfish. Respect the archaeological protection zone.

## Photography

The shallow depth and white sand bottom make the Windjammer ideal for wide-angle wreck photography. If a goliath grouper is present in the hull, stay calm and let it approach rather than chasing. The iron ribs silhouetted against the surface light provide strong compositional lines. Natural light is sufficient in shallow sections, but a strobe or video light brings out the orange of the gorgonians and the color of the encrusting corals.

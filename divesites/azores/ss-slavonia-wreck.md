# SS Slavonia Wreck

A 155-metre Cunard liner that ran aground off Flores in 1909 — widely cited as the first ship to transmit an SOS distress signal.

## Overview

The RMS Slavonia (originally SS Yamuna) was built in 1902 by Sir J. Laing & Sons at Sunderland. Originally laid down as the *Yamuna* for the British India Steam Navigation Company, she was purchased by Cunard Line before completion and renamed. At 10,606 GRT and 155 metres (510 ft) in length, she carried up to 71 first-class, 74 second-class, and 1,954 steerage passengers.

On 10 June 1909, she ran aground in foggy conditions at Ponta dos Fenais (Baixa Rasa), Flores, while en route from New York to the Mediterranean; Wikipedia specifies the captain was approximately 10.5 nautical miles off course and travelling at excessive speed for the conditions. All aboard were rescued without loss of life — *Prinzess Irene* took off 110 cabin-class passengers (84 of whom continued on to Naples) and *Batavia* took 300 steerage-class passengers; sources do not give a single confirmed total for crew present during the incident, though the ship's registered capacity included 225 crew. She was declared a total loss on 15 June (insured for £90,000). Some cargo was recovered: 400 bags of coffee, 1,000 copper ingots, 200 casks of oil, and agricultural machinery.

The SOS claim: multiple sources state the *Slavonia* sent the first maritime SOS signal in history. As of this audit (2026-07-11), Wikipedia's own article still flags this claim with a "better source needed" tag — the assertion remains widely repeated but not verified against a primary source, and the signal's use predated widespread adoption of SOS (CQD remained the Marconi standard in 1909).

The wreck lies at approximately 18 metres depth, less than 50 metres from the coast at Lajedo, Flores. It was designated an Underwater Archaeological Park in 2015. The wreck is substantially broken up after more than a century of wave action.

- Flores is remote; most visitors arrive by inter-island ferry or small plane
- The wreck is heavily broken up after 115+ years; remains scattered at 18 m

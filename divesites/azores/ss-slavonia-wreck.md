# SS Slavonia Wreck

A 155-metre Cunard liner that ran aground off Flores in 1909 — widely cited as the first ship to transmit an SOS distress signal.

## Overview

The RMS Slavonia (originally SS Yamuna) was laid down and launched on 15 November 1902 by Sir J. Laing & Sons at Sunderland (yard number 600), completing in June 1903. She was built as *Yamuna* for the British India Steam Navigation Company (originally 8,831 GRT) and was sold to Cunard Line in 1904, renamed *Slavonia*, and re-tonned to 10,606 GRT following her refit. At 155 metres (510 ft) in length, she carried up to 71 first-class, 74 second-class, and 1,954 steerage passengers after the Cunard refit.

On 10 June 1909, she ran aground in foggy conditions at Ponta dos Fenais, Lajedo, Flores, while en route from New York to the Mediterranean. Sources attribute the grounding to fog and the vessel running well off her intended course; the precise reasoning behind the course deviation is not conclusively documented and should not be presented as certain. All aboard were rescued without loss of life by the ships *Prinzess Irene* and *Batavia*. The total number of people aboard is genuinely unsettled across sources — Wikipedia's own figures for crew and passengers taken off by each rescue ship don't cleanly sum to a single total, and a separate figure of 597 appears in Azores government/press coverage. Rather than pick one number, this is best described as "sources report roughly 400 to over 600 people aboard, with no fatalities in any account." She was declared a total loss on 15 June. Cargo recovered included 400 bags of coffee, 1,000 copper ingots, 200 casks of oil, and agricultural machinery.

The SOS claim: multiple sources state the *Slavonia* sent one of the earliest, and possibly the first, maritime SOS distress calls. Wikipedia itself flags this claim as needing a stronger source, and the signal's use predated widespread adoption of SOS (CQD remained the more common Marconi standard in 1909). The claim is historically significant and widely repeated but not conclusively verified by a primary source — treat it as a notable but unconfirmed claim, not settled fact.

The wreck lies off Lajedo, Flores, at a depth reported as approximately 18 metres by prior research for this site; this figure could not be re-confirmed against a live, site-specific primary source in this pass (the dive.visitazores.com page previously cited for this site could not be found to still exist), so it is retained as the best available figure pending better sourcing rather than presented as fully verified. The site was designated an Underwater Archaeological Park in 2015 under Portuguese Regional Decree 17/2015/A (announced by the regional government in July 2015, published in the official journal that October) — confirmed by Público's contemporaneous coverage. The wreck is substantially broken up after more than a century of wave action.

## Marine Life

A search-indexed visitor review of the Flores dive operator running trips to this wreck mentioned lobsters, groupers, barracuda, and morays, but the underlying page could not be directly fetched to verify, so this is noted as low-confidence and not stated as fact. The scattered wreck structure provides artificial reef habitat typical of Azorean rocky coastline; regional species should not be presented as confirmed sightings at this specific site.

## Tips and Recommendations

- Historically notable: widely cited (though not fully verified) as one of the first ships to send an SOS signal, in 1909
- 155-metre Cunard liner, originally 8,831 GRT as built, later 10,606 GRT after refit — significant maritime heritage site
- Underwater Archaeological Park since 2015 (Portuguese Regional Decree 17/2015/A) — protected status
- Flores is remote; most visitors arrive by inter-island ferry or small plane
- The wreck is heavily broken up after 115+ years; depth and marine life details should be confirmed locally with a Flores-based operator before diving

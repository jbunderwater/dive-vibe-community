# SS Slavonia Wreck

A 155-metre Cunard liner that ran aground off Flores in 1909 — widely cited as the first ship to transmit an SOS distress signal.

## Overview

The RMS Slavonia (originally SS Yamuna) was built in 1902 by Sir J. Laing & Sons at Sunderland. Originally laid down as the *Yamuna* for the British India Steam Navigation Company, she was purchased by Cunard Line before completion and renamed. At 10,606 GRT and 155 metres (510 ft) in length, she carried up to 71 first-class, 74 second-class, and 1,954 steerage passengers.

On 10 June 1909, she ran aground in foggy conditions at Ponta dos Fenais (Baixa Rasa), Flores — Wikipedia attributes the grounding to the captain sailing roughly 10.5 nautical miles off course at excessive speed, reportedly to show passengers the coastal scenery. All aboard were rescued without loss of life: Wikipedia's account states the *Prinzess Irene* took off 110 cabin-class passengers and the *Batavia* took off 300 steerage-class passengers, with the ship's 225 crew leaving later the same day — a total of 635 people. Other secondary summaries of the same event give different, lower totals (approximately 600, or 410), so the exact headcount cannot be stated with certainty; the 635 figure is the one directly supported by Wikipedia's cited rescue narrative. She was declared a total loss on 15 June. Some cargo was recovered: 400 bags of coffee, 1,000 copper ingots, and 200 casks of oil.

The SOS claim: multiple sources (Wikipedia, Ocean Liners Magazine, British Newspaper Archive) state the *Slavonia* sent one of the earliest — commonly cited as the first — maritime SOS signals, received by the *Prinzess Irene* roughly 180 miles away. Wikipedia flags this claim with a "better source needed" note; SOS had only been formalized at the 1906 Berlin Radiotelegraphic Convention and CQD remained the more common Marconi distress call in 1909. The claim is historically significant and widely repeated but not conclusively verified by a primary source, so it is presented here as a commonly cited claim rather than an established fact.

The wreck lies at approximately 18 metres depth, less than 50 metres from the coast at Lajedo, Flores. It was designated an Underwater Archaeological Park in 2015. The wreck is substantially broken up after more than a century of wave action.

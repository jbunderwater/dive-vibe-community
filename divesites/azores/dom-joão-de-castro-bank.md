# Dom João de Castro Bank

The only seamount in the Atlantic with shallow-water hydrothermal activity — an underwater volcano between Terceira and São Miguel, now a Protected Marine Area.

## Overview

Dom João de Castro Bank is the only seamount in the Atlantic Ocean with energetic shallow-water hydrothermal activity and the only Atlantic seamount classified as a Protected Marine Area (IUCN wilderness designation, 2011, covering 16.31 km²). Located between São Miguel and Terceira islands on the Terceira Rift, the seamount rises from 1,000 metres depth to a summit 13 metres below the surface. At the summit sits a caldera approximately 300–600 metres in diameter, with the interior floor at 50 metres depth.

A fumarole field of approximately 1,600 m² occupies the northwestern sector of the caldera (16–45 m depth), releasing gas and warm fluid visible as bubbles from the surface. Scientific literature identifies two hydrothermal zones: a white zone with high hydrogen sulphide and methane content, and a yellow zone characterised by elevated iron and manganese. Reported temperatures vary considerably by source — a third independent value set was found on re-audit in addition to the two already noted, so the discrepancy is wider than previously stated: white-zone readings range from ~37°C to ~43°C across sources, and yellow-zone readings range from ~60°C to ~83°C. Given at least three diverging value sets in the literature, these should be treated as approximate ranges reflecting measurement methodology and temporal variability, not fixed figures.

An eruption in December 1720 briefly created an island — locally called 'Ilha Nova' (New Island) — approximately 1.5 km long and 250 m high. Sources disagree sharply on how quickly it eroded: Wikipedia and Azores tourism sites (dive.visitazores.com, Portugal Dive) state marine erosion destroyed the island within about two years (by mid-1722); however, a peer-reviewed academic source (Santos et al., "Spotlight: Dom João de Castro Seamount," *Oceanography*) states the cone "was eroded by ocean swells in just four months." This is an unresolved conflict between the tourism/encyclopedic sources and the primary scientific literature — readers should treat the erosion timeline as disputed (four months to roughly two years) rather than settled. Additional seismic activity was recorded in 1997. The 2.5–3 hour boat ride from Pico or Faial makes this an expedition dive.

## Site Information

- **Location**: Between São Miguel and Terceira, ~37 NM from Terceira Island
- **Entry Type**: Boat dive
- **Site Type**: Pinnacle/seamount
- **Difficulty Level**: Advanced
- **Maximum Depth**: 60 meters
- **Typical Visibility**: 30–50 meters (exceptional — fumarole bubbles enhance clarity)
- **Current**: Moderate to strong
- **Best Time**: June to September

## Marine Life

Wahoos, yellowmouth barracuda, Atlantic bonito, and Almaco jacks are confirmed by both dive.visitazores.com and Portugal Dive. Bermuda chubs and devil rays are confirmed by dive.visitazores.com and Wikipedia; loggerhead turtles by dive.visitazores.com and Portugal Dive; giant oceanic manta rays by dive.visitazores.com and Wikipedia. Spanish mackerel appears only in a single source (Portugal Dive) found in this pass and is flagged as unconfirmed by a second source. A unique hydrothermal-adapted mollusc community has been documented in peer-reviewed oceanographic literature. The commonly cited figure of ~220 species recorded in the broader bank area traces only to Wikipedia in this audit and could not be independently corroborated by a second source — treat as a single-sourced estimate.

## Tips and Recommendations

- The only shallow-water hydrothermal seamount in the Atlantic
- Two hydrothermal zones with visually distinct colours — white (high H₂S) and yellow (high Fe and Mn) — approximately 50 m apart; temperatures reported at roughly 37–43°C (white) and 60–83°C (yellow) across three diverging source value sets
- Protected Marine Area — no anchoring; mooring buoy required
- 2.5–3 hour boat ride from Pico or Faial — true expedition diving; weather-dependent
- The caldera erupted in 1720 and briefly formed a visible island ("Ilha Nova"); how quickly it eroded is disputed between sources (four months per academic literature vs. roughly two years per tourism/encyclopedic sources)

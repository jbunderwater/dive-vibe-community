## Akitsushima

The Akitsushima is Coron Bay's best-known WWII wreck — a Japanese Imperial Navy seaplane tender sunk by US carrier aircraft on 24 September 1944.

## Overview

Akitsushima was laid down on 29 October 1940, launched on 25 July 1941, and commissioned into the Imperial Japanese Navy on 29 April 1942 (Wikipedia). She was destroyed on 24 September 1944 when aircraft from US Task Force 38 attacked Coron Bay; dive-history accounts describe a direct hit aft that is believed to have detonated stored aviation fuel (scubatechphilippines.com, divezone.net). The wreck now lies on its port side. Sources report her maximum depth as between 36 and 38 meters, with the upper hull reaching up to about 22 meters — the discrepancy likely comes from different surveyed points on the broken hull. Her length is reported as approximately 114.8 meters by Wikipedia's technical specifications and around 118 meters by several dive-industry sources; standard displacement is reported at roughly 4,650 long tons. The ship's large seaplane-recovery crane, now resting on the sand, is the wreck's most recognisable feature and a common navigation landmark for divers.

## Site Information

- **Location**: Coron Bay, Palawan, Philippines
- **Entry Type**: Boat dive
- **Site Type**: Wreck dive
- **Difficulty Level**: Advanced
- **Maximum Depth**: 36 meters

## History

Akitsushima was the sole completed ship of her class, built as a large seaplane tender capable of servicing long-range flying boats. She was sunk during the US raid on Coron Bay on 24 September 1944 and struck from the naval registry on 10 November 1944. Beyond the attack date, unit, and general cause (bomb/strafing damage reported to have set off fuel stores), other narrative details of the sinking vary between sources and are not repeated here.

## Marine Life

Dive operators report barracuda, dogtooth tuna, batfish, groupers, and snappers around the wreck, though at least one operator notes fish density here is lower than at some of Coron's other wrecks. No source reviewed for this update confirmed sightings of any species not listed above at this specific site — regional species not attributed here should not be assumed to be present.

## Dive Profile

The wreck rests on its port side between roughly 22 and 38 meters. The crane and radio tower are accessible landmarks at shallower depth; the hull is reported to have split behind the mid-section, and trained wreck divers can penetrate to engine rooms containing several diesel engines and to compartments with deck-gun machinery. This is an advanced, often current-affected dive with typical visibility in the 5-15 meter range reported by local operators.

## Entry and Exit

Boat dive from Coron town operators; entry and exit are managed by the dive boat crew.

## Tips and Recommendations

- Full wreck penetration requires specific wreck-diving training and equipment — interior compartments are not a beginner or unguided activity
- Bring a dive light for the interior spaces and shaded areas around the crane
- Currents at this site are reported as moderate to strong; check conditions with your operator before descending

## Safety Considerations

Wreck penetration always carries risk, even for trained divers — silt-outs, entanglement, and loss of a direct exit route are real hazards inside any sunken hull. Dive within your training and certification limits, use a guide familiar with the wreck, and do not enter enclosed spaces without proper wreck or penetration certification and equipment (redundant air, line, lights).

## Akitsushima

The Akitsushima is Coron Bay's best-known WWII wreck, a Japanese Imperial Navy seaplane tender sunk by US carrier aircraft on 24 September 1944.

## Overview

The Akitsushima was laid down in October 1940 and launched in July 1941 as the sole completed ship of her class, built to service the Imperial Japanese Navy's large flying boats. She was sunk on 24 September 1944 during a US Task Force 38 air raid on Coron Bay and now lies on her port side between Lajo and Manglet islands, south of Concepcion village on Busuanga Island. Sources give slightly different figures for her size: naval-history references put her overall length at 114.8 meters, while dive-industry sources round this to about 118 meters; her standard displacement is reported at roughly 4,650 long tons. The wreck's shallowest point (the upper hull) sits at about 22 meters, with the deepest sections variously reported at 36-38 meters depending on the source. Her distinctive crane, once used to hoist seaplanes aboard, is one of the most recognizable features on the wreck, and advanced divers with wreck training can penetrate to the engine room to see her machinery.

## Site Information

- **Location**: Coron Bay, Palawan, Philippines
- **Entry Type**: Boat dive
- **Site Type**: Wreck dive
- **Difficulty Level**: Advanced
- **Maximum Depth**: 36 meters

## Marine Life

Two independent dive-site sources agree on barracuda and grouper being present around the wreck; one additionally reports tuna and batfish patrolling the structure (noting fish numbers are moderate rather than dense), while the other reports jacks, lionfish, mackerel, and scorpionfish. No source confirms sharks or turtles as regular sightings at this specific wreck.

## Dive Profile

Dives are typically planned to start near the bow, working toward the mast and crane, which are considered the highlight of the site. Penetration into the engine room, where all four engines are still visible, is possible for divers with wreck-diving certification but should not be attempted without proper training, lights, and a reel — interior spaces carry real risk regardless of experience level.

## Entry and Exit

Boat dive out into Coron Bay; entry and exit are typically via giant stride or back roll from a banca, with the wreck reached by descending a line to the upper hull structure.

## Safety Considerations

This is an advanced wreck dive with depths reaching the mid-to-high 30s in meters and areas of strong or unpredictable current around the crane and mast. Wreck penetration is never risk-free — even with proper certification and equipment, entanglement, silt-out, and disorientation are real hazards inside the hull. Dive within your training and always plan a clear exit route before entering enclosed spaces.

## Photography

The crane and mast make for the wreck's signature wide-angle shots, and the engine room machinery offers detail subjects for divers qualified to penetrate. A dive light is recommended for the interior spaces.

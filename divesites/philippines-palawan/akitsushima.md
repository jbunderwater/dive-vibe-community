## Akitsushima

Akitsushima is the most historically significant wreck in Coron Bay, a Japanese Imperial Navy seaplane tender sunk during a US air raid on 24 September 1944.

## Overview

The Akitsushima was laid down at Kawasaki's Kobe shipyard on 29 October 1940, launched 25 July 1941, and commissioned into the Imperial Japanese Navy on 29 April 1942 as one of only two purpose-built seaplane tenders in the fleet. She arrived at Coron Bay on 23 September 1944 and was sunk the following day by aircraft of US Task Force 38. Sources disagree on her exact hull length: Wikipedia, citing Imperial Japanese Navy specifications, lists 114.8 meters, while several Coron dive operators cite 118 meters. Displacement is more consistently reported at roughly 4,650 long tons. The wreck now lies on its port side, with the deepest section at 36 meters and the shallowest hull structure at 22 meters.

The ship's massive seaplane-handling crane rests visible on the wreck, and the hull is reported to be cracked open behind the mid-section, separating the crane and funnel area. Divers can see a 12.7cm gun turret and 25mm anti-aircraft guns near the funnel. Penetration into the lower decks and engine room, where four diesel engines remain intact, is possible for divers with wreck diving certification — this should never be treated as risk-free and requires proper training and equipment.

## Site Information

- **Location**: Coron Bay, Palawan, Philippines
- **Entry Type**: Boat dive
- **Site Type**: Wreck dive
- **Difficulty Level**: Advanced
- **Maximum Depth**: 36 meters

## Marine Life

Multiple independent accounts describe batfish and barracuda schooling around the crane and superstructure, with groupers, snappers, lionfish, and scorpionfish also commonly reported on the hull. Turtles and rays are occasionally seen. Coral and sponge growth on the wreck itself is comparatively sparse, with several sources noting the fish life — rather than coral cover — is the main draw.

## Dive Profile

Divers typically descend along the port-side hull to the crane and gun positions in the 22-30m range before continuing to the deepest sections near 36m. Because of the depth and potential for currents, this is considered an advanced dive; wreck penetration into the engine room requires specific wreck certification and should not be attempted without it.

## Safety Considerations

This is a deep, advanced wreck dive. Penetration of the interior — including the engine room — carries real risk of entanglement, silt-out, and disorientation and should only be attempted by divers with wreck-diving certification and appropriate redundant gas supply. It is never "safe," even for experienced divers, without that training.

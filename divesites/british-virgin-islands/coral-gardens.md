## Coral Gardens

A dual-attraction novice dive at Great Dog Island that pairs dense boulder coral formations with the fuselage of a deliberately sunk aircraft (a Short 330, per aviation records, though dive-tourism sources call it a "Shorts 360") — the wingless wreck has become a thriving artificial reef centerpiece. Note: a different, unrelated "Coral Gardens" reef site (no aircraft) exists off Dead Chest Island — see Overview for the naming conflict.

## Overview

Coral Gardens is tucked into the eastern tip of the south side of Great Dog Island in the British Virgin Islands (this database's coordinates, 18.480208, -64.456841, match Great Dog Island's published position almost exactly). **Note on naming**: there is a second, distinct BVI dive site also called "Coral Gardens" on the northeast side of Dead Chest Island (near Salt/Peter Island, well to the southwest) — a pure coral-reef site with no aircraft, described by BVI Diving's separate "diving-coral-gardens.html" page. That is a different site under the same name and is not the one documented here; this entry is specifically the Great Dog Island site.

The Great Dog Island site is known for two distinct features: extensive coral formations of boulder star coral, great star coral, brain corals, tube sponges, sea fans, and gorgonians; and the wreck of a twin-turboprop aircraft that rests on the sandy bottom at around 12–15 meters (40–50 ft). Dive-tourism sources (BVI Dive Map, BVI Diving) describe the aircraft as a "Shorts 360." However, two independent, non-tourism sources — Wikipedia's article on Terrance B. Lettsome International Airport and the Aviation Safety Network accident database — both identify it instead as a **Short 330-200, registration VP-LVR**, operated by Atlantic Air BVI. Given that these are specialist aviation-history/accident sources rather than dive-marketing copy, the Short 330 identification is treated as the more reliable one here; "Shorts 360" appears to be a transposition error that has propagated across dive sites. The aircraft aborted takeoff on 6 May 1993 and ran off the end of the runway into the sea (accounts vary on exact distance, roughly 60m/200ft off the runway); all 30 occupants (3 crew, 27 passengers) evacuated safely with no serious injuries. The airframe was later used as a prop in a BBC film production, then deliberately sunk here as part of the BVI's artificial reef program. The plane has no wings or tail section but has become, as BVI Dive Map notes, "the center of sea life, attracting even bigger fish." In December 2019, three additional aircraft (a Piper Navajo, Piper Aztec, and Cessna 150 — damaged in Hurricane Irma or a separate accident) were converted into shark-themed art installations ("Sharkplaneo": styled as a bull shark, a hammerhead, and a third shark species — sources are inconsistent on whether the third is a nurse shark or reef shark) and sunk nearby by the nonprofit Beyond the Reef as an environmental recycling/artificial-reef project. Mooring balls are available. Low swell and low current make this a comfortable novice-level dive.

## Site Information

- **Location**: South side of Great Dog Island, British Virgin Islands
- **Entry Type**: Boat dive
- **Site Type**: Wreck dive
- **Difficulty Level**: Beginner
- **Maximum Depth**: 15 meters
- **Depth Range**: 6–15 meters (20–50 feet)
- **Typical Visibility**: Good; clear water over sandy bottom
- **Current**: Low
- **Best Time**: Year-round

## Marine Life

Under the overhangs of the main wreck and coral formations: lobsters, spotted drums, grunts, and snappers. On the sand surrounding the wreck: stingrays, goatfish, lizardfish, and sand filefish. Schools of sennet frequent the area. Barracuda and occasional turtles are regularly sighted. The wreck structure itself shelters small reef fish and has accumulated significant coral and sponge encrustation since sinking.

## Dive Profile

Descend to the mooring line and approach the coral garden section first — the boulder and brain coral mounds are dense and worth exploring before moving to the wreck. The aircraft fuselage rests on the sand in the shallower part of the site; swim around the exterior and look into the open cabin for resident fish. Finish with the nearby "Sharkplane" installations in the sandy patches, then ascend gradually through the shallow coral zone for a safety stop before surfacing for boat pickup.

## Entry and Exit

Access is by dive boat. Mooring balls are available at the site. Entry via giant stride or back roll. Deploy an SMB for ascent signaling.

## Tips and Recommendations

- Explore the coral garden before the wreck to start the dive in the richest coral zone
- Bring a dive torch to illuminate the wreck interior and the overhangs where lobsters and spotted drums hide
- The "Sharkplane" art installations are in the sandy areas — look for them on a secondary pass
- Do not enter confined spaces inside the aircraft without proper training and equipment
- Maintain good buoyancy near the wreck to avoid disturbing accumulated silt

## Safety Considerations

Conditions are typically calm. Avoid stirring up silt inside the fuselage. Do not attempt wreck penetration beyond the open-door sections without proper certification and equipment. Always carry an SMB for ascent signaling.

## Photography

The aircraft fuselage makes a striking wide-angle subject, particularly when a diver is silhouetted at the cabin opening. The "Sharkplane" installations add unusual creative opportunities. Macro subjects abound on the sponge and coral-encrusted surfaces. Natural light is good in clear conditions; a torch helps in the overhangs.

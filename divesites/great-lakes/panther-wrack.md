## Panther (Wrack)

The Panther is a wooden steamer wreck in Lake Superior's Whitefish Bay, roughly six miles southeast of Whitefish Point, Michigan — not in the Straits of Mackinac or St. Marys River area as this profile previously (incorrectly) stated. On June 27, 1916, the downbound Panther collided with the upbound steel steamer James J. Hill in fog; the Hill's captain kept his bow wedged in the hole long enough for the entire Panther crew to escape before she sank.

## Overview

On June 27, 1916, the wooden steamer Panther (reported as either 237 or 249 feet in different sources) collided in fog with the 478-foot steel steamer James J. Hill. The Hill's captain, George Randolph, held his vessel's bow in the gash in the Panther's side until her crew had escaped across to the Hill's deck, then backed away; the Panther sank six miles southeast of Whitefish Point with no loss of life. The wreck was found in 1975 in 90-110 feet (27-34m) of water within the Whitefish Point Underwater Preserve. She sits upright and largely intact with her decks collapsed; two anchors are visible in the bow area along with grain cargo and tools in the stern. This part of Lake Superior — sometimes called the "Graveyard of Lake Superior" for its concentration of wrecks — is far less visited for dive tourism than Tobermory or Kingston, but rewards visitors with excellent visibility and cold, clear water.

## Entry and Exit

Dive charters from Paradise or Whitefish Point, Michigan provide access to the Whitefish Point Underwater Preserve. Infrastructure is less developed than at Tobermory or Kingston — plan accordingly. Entry by giant stride. Deploy SMB on ascent.

## Tips and Recommendations

- The Whitefish Point area rewards adventurous divers willing to explore off the beaten dive track
- Drysuit essential — Lake Superior water is cold year-round, especially at 27-34 metres
- The excellent visibility here compared to southern Great Lakes sites is a noticeable advantage
- The nearby Great Lakes Shipwreck Museum at Whitefish Point is well worth a visit
- At 27-34 metres, this site calls for solid buoyancy control and drysuit experience

## Safety Considerations

Remote location means longer emergency response times. Conservative dive planning and excellent equipment preparation are important. Lake Superior conditions can change quickly and the water is dangerously cold even in summer. Always dive with a buddy.

## Photography

The combination of excellent visibility and cold, clear water creates outstanding photography conditions at this depth. The hull's largely intact structure, visible anchors, and collapsed decks provide strong wide-angle subjects. The hull's natural appearance — less mussel colonized than southern Great Lakes wrecks — photographs differently and beautifully.

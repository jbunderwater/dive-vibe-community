# Mona Island

The "Galapagos of the Caribbean" — a remote, virtually uninhabited island in the Mona Passage between Puerto Rico and the Dominican Republic, with vertical walls, endemic species, and the most biodiverse diving in all of Puerto Rican waters.

## Overview

Mona Island lies at the midpoint of the Mona Passage, roughly 45–67 kilometres west of Mayagüez (sources vary on the exact distance) and closer to Hispaniola than to the Puerto Rican mainland. The island is protected as the Mona and Monito Islands Nature Reserve and designated a US National Natural Landmark (Wikipedia) — it has no permanent civilian population and requires advance permits from Puerto Rico's DRNA to visit or camp. A ScubaBoard trip report describes diving here as "cliff diving": swimming along sheer limestone walls past massive boulders and through swim-throughs, with fairly strong current and pronounced surge on most dives, and notes the site requires substantial prior experience — the consensus in that thread put a rough minimum around 50 logged dives. The island is home to endemic species including the Mona iguana. Access requires a multi-hour boat crossing from Mayagüez or Rincón — a day trip is not practical, so liveaboard or organised multi-day expeditions (e.g., Juliet Sailing & Diving) are the standard approach. Water temperature is 26–29°C.

## Marine Life

Wikipedia's entry on Mona Island confirms it hosts a large and growing hawksbill sea turtle rookery — over 1,500 clutches laid annually, one of the principal hawksbill nesting sites in the Caribbean. A ScubaBoard diver's trip report describes the diving as not especially "fishy" in volume but with larger individual specimens of common Caribbean reef fish than typical, attributed to the reserve's no-fishing protection; the same report notes fields of giant boulders, swim-throughs, and strong current and surge rather than naming pelagic species. Hard and soft corals, gorgonian sea fans, and black coral are documented on the wall. On this re-audit, hammerhead shark and manta ray sightings — carried in the previous version of this page — could not be confirmed against any source that specifically discusses Mona Island diving (Wikipedia, ScubaBoard, Carib Dive Guide, and Juliet Sailing & Diving's Mona program all omit them), and have been removed. Occasional reef shark sightings are reported in general Puerto Rico dive overviews but are not tied here to a single named source strong enough to state as expected; treat as possible rather than a guaranteed encounter. The previous mention of leatherback turtles nesting on Mona has also been removed — only hawksbill nesting is confirmed by the source found in this review.

## Dive Profile

Multiple wall sites surround Mona — the most commonly dived are on the calmer south and northwest sides. Descend from the reef crest to the wall, spending the deepest portion of the dive (30–40 metres) early before ascending the wall face. Each wall section has distinct character — the northwest side sees stronger current and more pelagic activity, the south side is calmer. Safety stop on the reef crest.

## Entry and Exit

Multi-day liveaboard or organised expedition from Mayagüez or Rincón. No day trips are practical — the crossing time and permit requirements make this a dedicated multi-day destination. Camping permits from DRNA are required for overnight stays. Entry by backward roll or giant stride from the expedition vessel. DSMB required.

## Tips and Recommendations

A permit from the Puerto Rico Department of Natural Resources is required for access — apply well in advance. The crossing is rough in winter swell; calm weather windows (May–August) are more reliable. Mona is a wilderness experience — no services exist on the island beyond the ranger station. Plan as a liveaboard or guided multi-day expedition. The combination of wall diving, snorkelling, and the extraordinary terrestrial wildlife (iguanas, seabirds) makes this one of the Caribbean's truly special destinations.

## Safety Considerations

The remote location means any diving incident is a serious logistical challenge. Dive conservatively with fully serviced equipment. Current in the Mona Passage can be significant — only dive in calmer conditions on the sheltered sides of the island when currents are manageable. DSMB required for all ascents.

## Photography

Mona's clean wall faces and boulder fields, in the visibility that gives the site its reputation, are the best-supported wide-angle subjects. Reef fish here tend to run larger than elsewhere in Puerto Rico (per ScubaBoard divers), which suits portrait shots. Do not plan a dive around hammerhead or manta encounters — no source found in this review confirms them at Mona.

## Additional Resources

- DRNA Puerto Rico: permits required for Mona access — apply at least 30 days in advance
- Taíno Divers (Rincón) and Caribbean Divers (Aguadilla): organise occasional multi-day Mona expeditions

## Gili Biaha

The most remote of the three Amuk Bay islands — famous for its Shark Cave and strong currents that attract advanced divers seeking white-tip reef sharks and seasonal mola mola.

## Overview

Gili Biaha is a small crescent-shaped island approximately 4 km northeast of Gili Tepekong, making it the outermost of the three islands off Padangbai in Amuk Bay. The island is surrounded by a dramatic black volcanic wall dropping to around 35–40 meters, with a signature cave feature at around 10–12 meters where white-tip reef sharks rest. Sources disagree on exactly where the cave sits relative to the island — reported variously as the northeastern side, the southwestern (sheltered) side, and the western face — so treat the compass direction as unconfirmed; all sources agree it is inside the more sheltered curve of the crescent. Divers report a distinctive pressure pulse felt in the chest when swell surges into the cave. The site is exposed to open ocean swells and the Indonesian Throughflow, creating strong and unpredictable currents, including up-currents and downcurrents, so negative-buoyancy descents straight to the wall are the norm. Dense coral coverage on the wall includes hard and soft corals, gorgonians, and sponges. Best visibility and conditions are from June to October, which also coincides with mola mola season.

## Site Information

- **Location**: Amuk Bay, east of Padangbai, east Bali
- **Entry Type**: Boat dive
- **Site Type**: Wall dive
- **Difficulty Level**: Advanced
- **Maximum Depth**: 40 meters
- **Typical Visibility**: 10–25 m
- **Current**: Strong and unpredictable; downcurrents possible
- **Best Time**: June to October

## Marine Life

White-tip reef sharks are the main attraction, frequently found resting inside the Shark Cave at around 10–12 meters. Schools of glass fish fill the cave entrance. Outside the cave, lobsters, octopuses, nudibranchs, leaf scorpionfish, pufferfish, turtles, and barracuda patrol the wall; barracuda and tuna sightings appear in multiple sources, though should be treated as occasional rather than guaranteed. From June to October, mola mola (oceanic sunfish) may be encountered, and occasional dolphin pods have also been reported. The site shares many of the same pelagic visitors as neighboring Gili Tepekong and Gili Mimpang.

## Dive Profile

Descend quickly to the wall using negative buoyancy to avoid being swept away by surface currents, entering on the sheltered side of the crescent. The Shark Cave is located at approximately 10–12 meters. After visiting the cave, follow the wall along the reef slope from 15–30 meters where gorgonians and larger marine life are concentrated. Allow the current to drift you along the wall. Ascend gradually and deploy an SMB well away from the island.

## Entry and Exit

Boat entry only, typically via jukung from Padangbai or Candidasa-area operators (roughly 20 minutes from Padangbai). Entry is normally a negative-buoyancy descent straight down to the wall to minimize time exposed to surface current. There is no shore access.

## Tips and Recommendations

- Dive with an operator experienced specifically at Biaha — conditions here are less forgiving than at neighboring Tepekong or Mimpang
- Expect a cold thermocline; bring more exposure protection than you would for other Bali sites
- Descend promptly on entry rather than lingering near the surface, where current and swell are strongest
- June to October offers the best combination of visibility and mola mola chances

## Safety Considerations

This is one of Bali's most demanding dive sites. Strong, unpredictable currents — including up-currents and downcurrents — are the primary hazards. Cold thermoclines can drop water temperature to around 20°C without warning. Advanced Open Water certification and significant drift diving experience are required; Deep Diver training is also recommended given the depths involved. Always dive with a local operator who knows the current conditions at Biaha.

## Photography

The Shark Cave is the signature wide-angle subject, with resting white-tips and backlit glassfish schools at the entrance. A fast, wide lens helps given the current — extended positioning for a shot is often not possible. Macro shooters can find nudibranchs and leaf scorpionfish on the wall outside the cave, though most divers prioritize wide-angle here given the conditions and limited bottom time near the cave.

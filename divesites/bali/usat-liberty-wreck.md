## USAT Liberty Wreck

One of the world's most accessible wreck dives — a WWII-era US Army cargo ship torpedoed on 11 January 1942, now lying parallel to shore just 25 metres offshore at Tulamben, accessible without a boat.

## Overview

The USAT Liberty was a US Army cargo transport launched on 19 June 1918 by the Federal Shipbuilding Company in Kearny, New Jersey. On 11 January 1942, at approximately 04:15, she was torpedoed roughly 10 nautical miles southwest of the Lombok Strait while en route from Tanjung Priok (Batavia) to the Philippines carrying rubber and railway parts (explosives are also recorded as deck cargo in wartime patrol records). The attacking submarine is recorded in most historical sources as I-66; that same vessel was administratively renumbered I-166 later in 1942, which is why some dive-tourism sources cite "I-166" for the January attack — both names refer to the same submarine, just from before and after its mid-1942 renumbering. All 54 aboard were rescued the same day by a Dutch flying boat. The US destroyer Paul Jones and Dutch destroyer Van Ghent attempted to tow the crippled ship toward Singaraja on Bali's north coast, but she was taking on too much water and was beached at Tulamben instead, around 14 January 1942, so her cargo and fittings could be salvaged. She sat on the beach for over two decades until tremors from the 1963 eruption of Mount Agung slid her into the sea, where she settled parallel to the shoreline and broke apart at the midsection.

Sources disagree on some of the vessel's exact particulars. Length is variously reported as 120 metres (several Tulamben dive operators) or 125 metres/411 ft 6 in (Wikipedia); tonnage figures also vary between roughly 6,211 gross tons (the figure used in wartime Japanese submarine attack records) and a Wikipedia-cited displacement of 13,130 tons — these may reflect different tonnage measures rather than a true contradiction, but no source reconciles them explicitly. Distance from shore is cited anywhere from about 10 to 40 metres depending on the source; the wreck is best described as lying a short swim from the black-sand beach, close enough that the shallowest sections are visible while snorkelling. The hull is now thickly encrusted in soft corals, gorgonian fans, and sponges, making it one of the world's most accessible and most-dived wrecks.

## Site Information

- **Location**: Tulamben, northeast Bali, Indonesia
- **Entry Type**: Shore entry
- **Site Type**: Wreck dive
- **Difficulty Level**: Beginner
- **Maximum Depth**: 30 meters
- **Typical Visibility**: 15–25 metres (up to 30m reported in the best conditions)
- **Water Temperature**: 26–30°C
- **Current**: Generally mild; can increase with tide
- **Best Time**: April–October for the most reliable visibility; August–September often cited as optimal

## Marine Life

Species reported specifically at the wreck (not just the wider Tulamben area) include schools of bigeye trevally, sweetlips, angelfish, and surgeonfish; hawksbill turtles and Napoleon wrasse; great barracuda; garden eel colonies in the sandy sections; bumphead parrotfish at dawn or on night dives; and green and spotted moray eels sheltering around the collapsed engine room and midsection. Reef sharks are reported occasionally but are not a reliable sighting. Macro subjects confirmed at the site include frogfish, ghost pipefish, and nudibranchs, with Spanish dancers, flashlight fish, and cleaner shrimp reported on night dives. Multiple independent operator sources corroborate this list; treat any additional species claims (e.g. mola mola or manta rays, which belong to Nusa Penida rather than Tulamben) with skepticism.

## Dive Profile

Depth increases from stern to bow. The stern section — with the rudder and prop visible — sits shallowest at roughly 5–9 metres and is a common starting point, followed by the midsection at 12–22 metres, where the hull separated during the 1963 eruption. The engine room and artillery/gun section lie in the 13–25 metre range; divers can make out the shape of boilers, gears, and a winch with steel cable beneath the coral growth, and a dive light helps pick out moray eels and flashlight fish in the darker recesses. Cargo hold areas are shallow enough (roughly 8–20 metres) to be explored by Open Water-certified divers without technical training. The bow, the deepest part of the wreck, is best suited to more experienced divers. Most dive operators plan a route from stern to bow (or the reverse) along one side of the hull and back along the other.

## Entry and Exit

Entry is from Tulamben's black volcanic-pebble beach; local porters commonly assist with carrying gear over the rocks. Divers wade in and swim a short distance (sources vary on the exact distance, roughly 10–40 metres) to reach the stern. Exit follows the same route back to the beach. The pebble beach can be slippery and awkward with camera gear — take it slowly and consider reef boots with good grip.

## Tips and Recommendations

- Morning dives (roughly 08:00–09:00) tend to have calmer seas and fewer divers
- Beginners should focus on the stern and cargo hold sections; save the bow and engine room for when you're comfortable managing depth and buoyancy
- Bring a dive light even for daytime dives — it brings out the color of the coral growth and helps spot moray eels and macro life in the wreck's recesses
- Maintain careful buoyancy around silty interior sections to avoid stirring up sediment and reducing visibility for other divers
- Combine with the adjacent Drop-off (Tulamben Wall) and Coral Garden Tulamben for a full day of diving without changing entry points

## Safety Considerations

This is a broken-up, open wreck rather than an intact vessel, and much of it can be seen without entering enclosed spaces. Divers who do want to look inside partially enclosed sections such as the engine room should treat this as an overhead-environment activity: proper wreck training, a dive light, and careful buoyancy control are necessary, and entry into any enclosed space should never be treated as risk-free — sediment can reduce visibility to zero quickly. Boat traffic runs close to shore at this site; use a surface marker buoy and stay aware of your surroundings on ascent.

## Photography

Wide-angle is the standard choice for capturing the coral-encrusted hull, schooling bigeye trevally, and diver-with-wreck compositions. A dive light is useful even in daylight to restore true color to the coral growth and to pick out subjects in the engine room. Macro shooters can look for frogfish, ghost pipefish, and nudibranchs on the hull and surrounding sand, with Spanish dancers and flashlight fish as night-dive subjects.

## Pyramids

Amed's iconic artificial reef — more than 20 hollow concrete pyramid structures placed by local fishermen as a reef-enhancement effort, now encrusted with soft corals, sea fans, and teeming with reef fish.

## Overview

The Pyramids dive site near Jemeluk Bay in Amed was created when local fishermen placed more than 20 hollow concrete pyramid structures on the black sand bottom at approximately 12–25 metres as a reef enhancement effort. Sources disagree on exactly when: some operator sites cite "the early 1990s," while others describe it only as "more than 20 years ago" without naming a decade — since this is a historical claim without two independently corroborating primary sources, treat the specific "early 1990s" date as unconfirmed rather than established fact. What is well corroborated is that the structures are now covered in soft corals, sea fans, and hard coral growth, and are teeming with reef fish. The site functions as a resting/sheltering spot where hawksbill and green turtles are regularly seen. Blue-spotted stingrays rest on the surrounding sandy floor. Moderate current can sweep through the area on some days, enabling drift-style dives with good visibility.

## Site Information

- **Location**: Near Jemeluk Bay, Amed, northeast Bali
- **Entry Type**: Shore or boat entry
- **Site Type**: Reef
- **Difficulty Level**: Intermediate
- **Maximum Depth**: 22 meters
- **Typical Visibility**: 20–30+ m (current-swept)
- **Current**: Moderate to strong; drift possible
- **Best Time**: April to November

## Marine Life

Hawksbill and green turtles are regularly seen sheltering along the pyramids and slope (the nearby snorkel area is locally known as "Turtle Point"). Blue-spotted stingrays rest on the sandy floor between structures, and garden eels colonize the surrounding sand. Leaf scorpionfish and frogfish are reported in and around the pyramid structures, and pygmy seahorses have been found on the site's gorgonian/sea fans. Titan triggerfish, moray eels, oriental sweetlips, butterflyfish, blueface angelfish, and lionfish are common. Occasional larger visitors reported by local operators include giant barracuda and reef sharks. Nudibranchs are numerous in the rubble around the base of each pyramid.

## Dive Profile

The site spans 5–20 m with different pyramid clusters at various depths. Begin in the shallower zone at 5–10 m and work progressively deeper across the site. Spend time at each pyramid examining its micro-habitats. Nitrox extends bottom time significantly at 18–20 m where the main clusters are located. Drift dives are possible when current is running — start at the east end and drift west.

## Entry and Exit

Boat dive with local Amed operators (under 5 minutes from the dive centre). Can also be dived as a shore dive with a swim-out. Entry via giant stride or back roll. Follow the guide's current direction briefing for correct orientation.

## Tips and Recommendations

- Nitrox is highly recommended — the pyramids reward slow, thorough exploration at 20 m
- Check every pyramid structure systematically; each is its own micro-ecosystem with different critters
- The sandy floor between pyramids hosts blue-spotted stingrays in remarkable numbers
- Come at low tide if possible — clearer, faster current improves visibility
- Night dives on the pyramids reveal completely different critters including crabs, shrimps, and hunting octopus

## Safety Considerations

Strong current can make it difficult to hold position at the deeper pyramids. Be prepared to drift if current is running and avoid fighting it. Stonefish on the sandy floor are well-camouflaged — do not stand on the sand. Blue-spotted stingrays have venomous tails; observe without disturbing them. Always carry an SMB for drift dive pickups.

## Photography

The pyramids are a macro photographer's paradise — soft corals, frogfish, pygmy seahorses, and nudibranchs all on a single geometric structure. Wide-angle shots capturing turtles on the coral-covered pyramids are the signature image. Blue-spotted stingrays in groups on the black sand are striking wide-angle subjects.

## Norwegian Bay

Norwegian Bay is a remote, shallow-lagoon dive and snorkel area approximately 80 kilometres north of Coral Bay, accessible only by liveaboard sailing vessel, and home to the ruins of a historic shore-whaling station and associated shipwrecks.

## Overview

Norwegian Bay was the site of a shore-based whaling station, established in 1915 by a Norwegian whaling company operating steam-powered chasers, which caught over 4,000 whales in its first four seasons (1913–1916). The Australian North-West Whaling Company purchased the station in 1920 and ran three whale chasers — the Fynd, Fin, and Frey. The station was reopened in 1949 and operated until 1957, when whaling operations relocated to Babbage Island near Carnarvon. The foundations, flensing decks, slipways, and rusted machinery remain accessible on shore.

Three wrecks lie offshore in the bay, and their histories have been cross-checked against the WA Museum's Maritime Archaeology Database (the state's authoritative shipwreck register) and independent dive/wreck sites:

- **The Fin** — an iron whaler built in Norway in 1881, of 93 gross tons. The WA Museum's official record states the vessel was "at anchor, driven ashore," while dive-tourism sources (Sail Ningaloo, Diving WA Wrecks) describe it as wrecked in a cyclone in 1923; both point to a storm-related loss but the exact circumstances are not fully reconciled between sources. The museum record also notes unresolved research (Stanbury, 1984) into whether the wreck is actually the Fin or her sister ship, the Fynd — the two vessels have historically been confused. The wreck lies in shallow water and is a popular, easy snorkel in clear water within the Norwegian Bay sanctuary zone.
- **The Zvir** — a steel screw steamer built in 1900 by Priestman & Co. of Sunderland, UK, 100.2 metres long and 3,353 gross tons, registered in Rijeka (then Fiume), Austria-Hungary. She ran aground at Point Cloates on 27 November 1902 while carrying a cargo of sugar from Java to Port Adelaide; all 21 crew survived. Lying at roughly 10 metres depth, the wreck retains an upright propeller, engine block, boilers, and deck winches, and is considered one of the cleanest, most intact wreck sites on the WA coast. Discovered by skin divers in 1978, it is a federally protected heritage site.
- **The SS Perth** — an iron passenger/cargo steamer built in 1863 in Glasgow, Scotland (58.5 metres, approximately 499 gross tons), operated by the Adelaide Steamship Company under Captain Fred Parkes. She struck Ningaloo Reef on 17 September 1887 while sailing from Fremantle to Wyndham carrying 63 passengers and general cargo; no casualty figures are recorded in the sources reviewed. Most of the wreckage lies in very shallow water and is exposed to swell, making it a difficult site to access.

An iron whale-oil barge approximately 12 metres in length also rests 100 metres offshore, north of the old jetty (per divingwawrecks.com).

The diving and snorkelling here is shallow — typically 3–4 metres over sandy lagoons and fringing reef — and the area's remoteness means minimal visitor pressure and pristine conditions. Sail Ningaloo operates 5-night liveaboard tours that include Norwegian Bay as a destination.

## Site Information

- **Location**: Norwegian Bay, Ningaloo Marine Park, Western Australia (~80km north of Coral Bay; liveaboard access only)
- **Entry Type**: Boat dive
- **Site Type**: Wreck dive
- **Difficulty Level**: Beginner
- **Maximum Depth**: 4 meters
- **Wrecks present**: Fin (iron whaler, built 1881, 93 tons — sources disagree on whether this vessel is the Fin or sister ship Fynd, and on cyclone vs. driven-ashore sinking cause), Zvir (steel steamer, built 1900, 3,353 tons, wrecked 1902), SS Perth (iron steamer, built 1863, ~499 tons, wrecked 1887), Norwegian Bay whale-oil barge (~12m)
- **Access**: Sail Ningaloo 5-night liveaboard tour; no day-boat access

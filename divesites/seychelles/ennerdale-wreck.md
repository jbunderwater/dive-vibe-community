## Ennerdale Wreck

The Ennerdale is the largest shipwreck in the Seychelles, a Royal Fleet Auxiliary tanker that struck an uncharted granite pinnacle off Mahé in June 1970 and now rests in sections northeast of Victoria.

## Overview

RFA Ennerdale (A213) was a Dale-class mobile reserve tanker originally named *Naess Scotsman*, built in 1962–63 at Kieler Howaldtswerke AG in Kiel, Germany (Wikipedia and the Historical RFA Association both confirm this builder, though the two sources give slightly different launch years — 1962 per one record, 1963 per another — so her exact launch date is treated here as uncertain within that range). She was acquired by the British government in 1968. On 1 June 1970, while departing Mahé after a crew rest period during Beira Patrol duties, she struck an uncharted underwater granite obstruction — described variously as a "pinnacle of rock" (Historical RFA) or a "coral reef" (Wikipedia) — off the northeast coast of the island, holing her below the waterline. She began taking on water and sank within approximately 30 minutes. Her full load of oil created an environmental emergency; the clean-up operation involved detonating torpedo warheads placed on the hull by helicopter to rupture the oil tanks, with six of ten warheads reportedly fired to help the South-East Monsoon disperse the spill away from Mahé.

**Ship dimensions are genuinely disputed across sources and are reported here as a range rather than a single figure.** The Historical RFA Association and English Wikipedia both give a length of 710 feet (216–220 metres depending on rounding) with a tonnage of 29,189 GRT (gross register tonnage) and 49,209–62,000 long tons of displacement (sources differ on whether the larger figure is displacement or deadweight). A Seychelles dive-tourism guide (koek.sc), by contrast, describes the wreck as a "196-metre, 26,000-tonne" tanker, and theDiveGlobe describes her as "62,000-ton." Given this spread, this page reports the ship as roughly 196–220 metres in length and in the high tens-of-thousands of tons, without asserting a single precise figure. What is consistently confirmed across sources: she was, by a wide margin, the largest wreck in Seychelles waters.

The wreck now lies broken into sections as a result of the explosive demolition. Dive-specific sources are reasonably consistent on depth: theDiveGlobe cites a maximum of 30m and average of 25m, while koek.sc-style operator descriptions place the shallowest superstructure around 18m with deeper stern sections at 30–35m — consistent with this page's existing 30m maximum-depth figure. The stern section is reported as the most intact and impressive part of the site, with the midship section having collapsed during salvage operations; the propeller and prop shaft remain recognisable. Interior penetration is not recommended without appropriate wreck training and equipment, and carries inherent risk regardless of experience level.

Marine life reported at the wreck includes giant groupers, moray eels, and dense schools of barracuda and fusiliers, now colonizing a structure heavily encrusted with soft corals, sea fans, and sponges after more than fifty years underwater. Reef sharks (whitetip and grey reef) and Napoleon wrasse have also been reported. Best diving conditions are October to May.

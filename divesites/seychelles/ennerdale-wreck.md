## Ennerdale Wreck

The Ennerdale is the largest shipwreck in the Seychelles, a Royal Fleet Auxiliary tanker that struck an uncharted granite pinnacle off Mahé in June 1970 and now rests in around 30 metres of water northeast of Victoria.

## Overview

RFA Ennerdale (pennant A213) was a Dale-class mobile reserve tanker originally launched as *Naess Scotsman* on 30/31 August 1963, entering charter service with the UK government from July 1967. Note there is a second, unrelated ship also named RFA Ennerdale (A173) — an earlier Dale-class tanker scrapped in 1959–60 — and this destination's wreck is confirmed to be the later A213 vessel. On 1 June 1970, while departing Mahé to resume refuelling duties along the East African coast after a crew rest period, she was holed below the waterline by an uncharted granite obstruction in the sheltered waters off Victoria and sank with a full load of oil within roughly 30 minutes.

**Ship dimensions — sources conflict.** Wikipedia's dedicated infobox for RFA Ennerdale (A213) gives a length of 710 ft (216.4 m) and a displacement of 62,000 long tons (29,189 GRT); theDiveGlobe's dive-site guide independently cites the same "62,000-ton" figure, which is the best-corroborated tonnage across the sources checked. However, other dive-focused sources give markedly different numbers: Divebooker states 74,000 tons, and Koek.sc's wreck-diving guide states 196 metres in length and 26,000 tonnes. Rather than pick one figure, the range across all sources checked is: length approximately 196–216 metres, tonnage approximately 26,000–74,000 tons/tonnes depending on source and whether displacement or a different tonnage measure is meant. The Wikipedia/theDiveGlobe figures (216 m, 62,000 tons) have the strongest independent corroboration of the set.

Because salvage of the intact, oil-laden hull was not possible, the vessel was deliberately destroyed with explosives — accounts describe torpedo warheads and anti-submarine mortar bombs placed on the hull by helicopter, since strong currents ruled out diver placement — to rupture the tanks and let the South-East Monsoon disperse the oil away from Mahé. The exact count and date of the explosive charges used could not be confirmed from more than one source this round and are not repeated here. The explosive destruction scattered much of the hull, and the wreck now lies in sections; sources report the shallowest superstructure at around 18–20 metres and deeper sections down to 28–35 metres. The stern section is consistently described as the most intact and impressive part of the site, with the propeller and prop shaft recognisable. Interior penetration is not recommended without appropriate wreck training and equipment — this remains a broken, disorienting structure, not a safe swim-through.

Marine life reported at the site includes giant groupers, moray eels, dense schools of fusiliers, and barracuda, plus whitetip and grey reef sharks and hawksbill turtles using the hull as a cleaning and resting point (koek.sc); one source also lists nurse sharks. Eagle rays and large pelagics pass regularly over the scattered hull plates, and the structure is heavily encrusted with soft corals, sea fans, and sponges. Best diving conditions are reported as October to May.
